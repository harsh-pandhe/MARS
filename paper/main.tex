\begin{abstract}
%==============================================================================
We describe MARS (Multi-Agent Robot Swarm), an open ROS\,2 Jazzy / Gazebo Harmonic testbed for
cooperative area coverage by a small team of differential-drive robots. It puts
three controllers behind one three-stage safety stack (two fixed override
rules, a soft control-barrier-function filter, and a front safeguard): a
classical decentralized planner (frontier selection, A* routing, Voronoi
partitioning with consensus-based auction claims within a $3\,\si{m}$ communication
range, and a behavior tree), a random walk, and a MAPPO policy with a
permutation-invariant neighbor encoder and a centralized critic, across five
Gazebo worlds. In long single reference runs the classical planner reached
high discovered-map ACR (85.7--100\% in four worlds, 25.0\% in the maze),
and in an exploratory single-episode matrix a trained MAPPO checkpoint had
lower observed discovered-map ACR than the classical planner in all five worlds; the
comparison is between an engineered system and a minimally trained reactive
policy, not a matched-information benchmark. We also document three limits of the testbed: evaluation of an identical checkpoint
varies between runs (a documented property that users must account for),
the safety margin is soft with no formal guarantee, and the architecture's
transfer to other team sizes is untested. Code and per-run results for the reported
experiments are archived with a DOI.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
multi-robot systems, area coverage, control barrier functions, MAPPO,

\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
%==============================================================================
Cooperative area coverage, in which a team of robots jointly sweeps an unknown
environment, underlies search, inspection and monitoring. Evaluating
coordination methods for it needs a simulator that models range sensing and
contact, and results that others can reproduce. MARS is a testbed built for
that purpose. It wraps Gazebo in a multi-agent environment, provides a
classical decentralized controller and a learned MAPPO baseline behind the
same safety stack, and archives per-run results.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A documented testbed with five worlds, three controllers and a common
        safety stack, described here as implemented (Sec.~\ref{sec:testbed}).
  \item Long-run reference coverage of the classical controller in the five worlds, a
        robot-count sweep, and a comparison with trained MAPPO baselines, all
        from archived runs (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item An account of the testbed's limits: non-deterministic evaluation, a
        soft safety margin, and claims that remain untested
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}).
\end{itemize}
We present the baselines as a starting point, not as state-of-the-art ceilings, and leave open for users the questions of competitive learned policies, a safety filter with a formal guarantee, and transfer across team sizes.

\section{Related Work}
%==============================================================================
Frontier-based exploration~\cite{yamauchi1997frontier} and its multi-robot
extensions~\cite{burgard2005coordinated,amigoni2017multirobot} are the
classical baseline for this task; the consensus-based bundle
algorithm~\cite{choi2009cbaa} supplies the auction mechanism we use to resolve
duplicate target claims. MAPPO~\cite{yu2022mappo} is a strong baseline for
cooperative multi-agent learning; we train it with
RLlib~\cite{liang2018rllib}. The actor's neighbor encoder is a mean-pooled
embedding in the style of Deep Sets~\cite{zaheer2017deepsets}; it contains no
message passing, so we do not call it a graph neural network. Control barrier
functions~\cite{ames2019cbf} give a quadratic-program safety filter, and
barrier-function filters have been placed around learned
policies~\cite{cheng2019end}. We solve ours with OSQP~\cite{stellato2020osqp}
and add a slack variable, which makes it soft (Sec.~\ref{sec:safety}). A
companion study~\cite{pandhe2026mappo} examines the MAPPO baseline in depth.

\section{The Testbed}
\label{sec:testbed}
%==============================================================================
\subsection{Platform and Metrics}
TurtleBot3 Waffle robots (differential drive, 24-sector LiDAR, odometry), instantiated in teams of $N\in\{2,3,5,8\}$ for the classical-controller sweep and $N=3$ for the learned experiments,
run in Gazebo Harmonic under ROS\,2 Jazzy~\cite{macenski2022ros2,gazebo}. The
simulator is wrapped as a PettingZoo~\cite{terry2021pettingzoo} parallel
environment, so any code that speaks the standard multi-agent parallel API can drive the simulator without touching ROS\,2 internals; our own training uses it through RLlib. The workspace is a grid of $0.4\,\si{m}$ cells. A cell is marked
visited when a robot's pose falls in it or a LiDAR ray (up to $2.0\,\si{m}$)
passes through it as free space. The \emph{area-coverage rate} is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ACR}=\frac{|\text{visited}\setminus\text{obstacle}|}
{N_{\text{cells}}-|\text{obstacle}|}\times100,
\end{equation}
where ``obstacle'' cells are those the robots have registered as obstacles, so
the denominator depends on what has been discovered. ACR here is therefore a \emph{discovered-map} metric: controllers that discover obstacles at different rates have different denominators, so ACR is not a fixed-denominator measure of physical coverage, and a controller that discovers few obstacles keeps a larger denominator (lowering its ACR), while one that finds obstacles quickly, for example by hugging walls, shrinks it early. A ground-truth-denominator metric is not reported (Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}). A \emph{wall-proximity event} is
counted when the minimum LiDAR range is below $0.14\,\si{m}$ (the approximate
sensor-to-bumper distance); an \emph{inter-robot proximity event} when two robots are closer
than $0.20\,\si{m}$ after the first 15 steps. Events are counted per robot per control step. Robots start from fixed spawn positions on a line 0.7\,m apart (offsets $0,-0.7,+0.7,-1.4,\dots$\,m along one axis from the world's spawn point), identical in every episode. These are threshold-based sensor proxies, not verified physical contacts. The inter-robot threshold is
smaller than the filter's separation buffer (Sec.~\ref{sec:safety}), so a zero count
does not show that the margin held.

\subsection{Observation and Action}
Each robot observes a 46-vector: 24 LiDAR sector ranges, goal distance and
bearing, linear and angular velocity, and nine neighbor slots of (distance,
bearing), padded with $(10.0,0.0)$. Actions are $(v,\omega)$ with
$|v|\le0.22\,\si{m/s}$ and $|\omega|\le1.0\,\si{rad/s}$. Because the vector has
a fixed length, the network accepts up to nine neighbors.

\subsection{Safety Stack}
\label{sec:safety}
Every controller's command passes through three stages.
\emph{(1) Override rules.} If the minimum LiDAR range over all beams is below
$0.22\,\si{m}$ and the commanded forward speed is positive, forward speed
becomes $-0.08\,\si{m/s}$; if another robot is within $0.45\,\si{m}$ and within
$72^\circ$ of the heading, forward speed becomes $-0.05\,\si{m/s}$.
\emph{(2) Soft CBF.} A quadratic program (OSQP) solves
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}\min_{v,\omega,\delta}\ &(v-v_{\text{nom}})^2+0.05(\omega-\omega_{\text{nom}})^2+P\delta^2\\ \text{s.t.}\ &A_kv+B_k\omega+\delta\ge-\gamma h_k,\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $-0.22\le v\le0.22$, $-1.0\le\omega\le1.0$ (the action bounds, enforced as variable bounds), $0\le\delta\le10$, $P=1000$ and $\gamma=2.0$. Barrier terms come from LiDAR
returns within $0.6\,\si{m}$, evaluated at two lookahead points ($\pm0.12\,\si{m}$
on the heading axis) with obstacle radius $0.20\,\si{m}$, and from neighbors
within $1.2\,\si{m}$ with a separation buffer of $0.45\,\si{m}$ (not a physical radius). The neighbor term treats
the neighbor's velocity as bounded and unmodeled. If the QP is infeasible the
filter returns $(\min(0,v_{\text{nom}}),\omega_{\text{nom}})$.
\emph{(3) Front safeguard.} Forward speed is set to $\min(0,v)$ if any of the
five forward-cone beams reads below $0.24\,\si{m}$.
The bounded slack can relax conflicting constraints when the required relaxation is within its bound (otherwise the QP is infeasible and the fallback above is used), at the price of the guarantee: because of $\delta$, the radii are soft constraints: we claim no
formal guarantee and report behaviour only.

\subsection{Classical Controller}
Each robot keeps its own visited and obstacle grids, built from its LiDAR. The
stack has these components:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Map sharing:} robots within $3.0\,\si{m}$ merge visited grids by
        bitwise OR, a range-limited approximation of gossip consensus; a global
        grid, accumulated from all robots' LiDAR returns and not exposed to the controllers, is kept only for monitoring and for computing ACR; its obstacle set is the ACR denominator's obstacle set.
  \item \textbf{Target selection:} frontier cells are the nearest unvisited
        candidates inside the robot's own Voronoi cell relative to sensed
        neighbors; duplicate claims are resolved by a consensus-based auction among
        peers in range, and a stale claim from a peer that has left range
        expires after 30 steps. Unreachable targets are blacklisted.
  \item \textbf{Routing:} 8-connected A* on an obstacle-inflated grid.
  \item \textbf{Behavior tree:} in priority order, mission-complete standby or
        perimeter patrol, an escape maneuver ($v=-0.12\,\si{m/s}$,
        $\omega=\pm0.6\,\si{rad/s}$) when a robot has not moved for 30 steps,
        frontier exploration, and a boundary-aware wander.
  \item \textbf{Localization:} a correlative scan matcher corrects odometry drift.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Random-Walk Controller}
At each step every robot's command is drawn uniformly from the action box $[-0.22,0.22]\times[-1.0,1.0]$ (the environment's \texttt{action\_space.sample()}), independently per robot and step, with no observation or goal information, and then passes through the same three-stage safety stack. The Gazebo seed is fixed (42); the action sampler's RNG state was not retained, so Random-Walk results are one stochastic realization rather than a reproducible seeded baseline. It is included as a minimally informed behavioral baseline, not an information-matched one.

\subsection{MAPPO Baseline}
A single policy is shared by all robots. Each neighbor's (distance, bearing)
pair is embedded by a two-layer ReLU network ($2\to16\to16$), masked and
mean-pooled, concatenated with the 28 ego features and passed through a
two-layer Tanh actor ($44\to64\to64$). A centralized critic ($76\to64\to64\to1$,
Tanh) takes the same 44 features plus a mean-pooled embedding
($48\to32\to32$) of the other agents' observation--action pairs. The
mean-pooled embedding is invariant to neighbor order and padding position, which
\texttt{tests/test\_permutation\_invariance.py} checks directly. Training uses
RLlib PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} with a single in-process rollout worker,
train batch 700, $90$-step episodes, minibatch 64, 5 epochs, learning rate
$10^{-4}$, clip 0.2, $\gamma=0.99$, GAE $\lambda=0.95$ and entropy
coefficient $0.01$. The per-step reward is
\begin{multline}
r_i=-0.01+5.0\,\Delta d_i+4.0\,n_{\text{new}}+20\,\mathbb{I}[\text{goal}]\\
-8.0\,\mathbb{I}[\text{wall}]-\rho_i,
\end{multline}
where $\Delta d_i$ is the reduction in goal distance, $n_{\text{new}}$ the number
of cells newly visited by any robot this step, and $\rho_i=0.5\,\mathbb{I}[d_{\min,i}<0.5\,\si{m}]+10\,\mathbb{I}[d_{\min,i}<0.20\,\si{m}]$ with $d_{\min,i}$ the distance to the nearest other robot. The discovery term $n_{\text{new}}$ is a team-level quantity used only in the training reward; it is not part of the decentralized observation. The goal is a random
point from a fixed per-world list of safe positions, reassigned on arrival; it
is less informed than the classical controller's map-aware frontier targets. The policy is purely reactive: it has no recurrent state or local map, and no spatial-memory variant was trained.
Evaluation uses greedy actions. All learned results use a single archived checkpoint (\texttt{checkpoints/mappo\_baseline}, trained on the cafe); the training seed and iteration count of that checkpoint are not recorded in its archived metadata and no checkpoint-selection procedure was applied, so no across-seed estimate is claimed for it. The checkpoint is identified by the SHA-256 of its policy state file, \texttt{f07518b9\allowbreak 1630cb0e\allowbreak d153109d\allowbreak a320ae94\allowbreak 96a0f8dd\allowbreak 008c0448\allowbreak 6add1cfb\allowbreak 2aab6edb}.

\textbf{Protocol and caveats.} Unless stated, each cell is one episode. We
found that repeating an evaluation of an identical checkpoint and seed gives
different outcomes (Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}), so single-episode numbers carry
unquantified run-to-run variance; we treat them as descriptive. Raw per-run
records are archived (Data availability).

\subsection{Safety Observations}
Across these heterogeneous exploratory runs (20 sweep runs, 25 matrix runs and a 27-episode ablation, 72 in total), inter-robot proximity events were absent in 69 of 72, while coverage
ranged from 1.1\% to 98.6\% (across worlds and horizons), so the event count did not distinguish weak
from strong controllers. In the office run the minimum inter-robot distance
was $0.38\,\si{m}$, below the $0.45\,\si{m}$ CBF separation buffer, with no proximity event,
consistent with the intended behaviour of a soft safety stack. Two one-off qualitative stress tests with moving hazards (head-on at $0.38\,\si{m/s}$ and crossing at $0.22\,\si{m/s}$) were
run once each without a proximity event; their raw traces are not archived.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
%==============================================================================
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Non-deterministic evaluation.} Three evaluations of one saved
        checkpoint with the same seed argument gave coverage between 24.6\%
        and 37.8\%, and proximity-event counts of 0 to 19; the cause was not isolated: greedy actions remove policy sampling noise, and we suspect simulator or runtime timing as a contributor, but physics scheduling, message ordering and reset state were not separately tested. The three repeats used one checkpoint on the cafe for 150 steps. Single-episode cells
        (Tables~\ref{tab:sweep} and~\ref{tab:mx}) are descriptive only.
  \item \textbf{Soft safety margin; no formal guarantee.} See
        Sec.~\ref{sec:safety}. We did not measure how often or how much the
        override rules and filter change each controller's commands, so reported outcomes are those of controller plus safety stack, not of the controller alone.
  \item \textbf{ACR is a discovered-map metric.} Its denominator excludes only cells where a LiDAR return has registered an obstacle, so values are not directly comparable across controllers that discover obstacles at different rates, and 100\% means all currently non-obstacle cells were visited, not that all physically navigable area was verified. A ground-truth-denominator ACR is future work.
  \item \textbf{Transfer to other team sizes is untested.} The encoder accepts
        up to nine neighbors and is order-invariant, but no trained policy was
        evaluated with a different number of robots. The sweep uses the
        classical controller.
  \item \textbf{Ablations not run.} We did not ablate the CBF, the consensus
        merge, the behavior tree or the scan matcher, so we cannot say how
        much each contributes.
  \item \textbf{Scope.} Simulation only, one robot model, three robots in the
        learned experiments, one training world, short training (about
        $3\times10^4$ steps), and no comparison with other published
        coverage planners.
\end{itemize}

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%==============================================================================
MARS provides a documented testbed with archived code and per-run results. In the
reported long reference runs the classical controller's discovered-map ACR
was high in four of five worlds, observed discovered-map ACR generally increased with
team size in the exploratory sweep, and in the single-episode matrix the
observed MAPPO discovered-map ACR was lower than the classical controller's in all five
worlds. These results are descriptive: evaluation is not deterministic, the
learned and classical controllers differ in information and design, the safety
margin is soft, and several architectural claims remain untested. We release
the code, per-run logs and scripts so that others can check and extend them.
