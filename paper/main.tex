\begin{abstract}
%==============================================================================
We describe MARS, an open ROS\,2 Jazzy / Gazebo Harmonic testbed for
cooperative area coverage by a small team of differential-drive robots, and
report what it showed. Three controllers share one safety stack (two fixed
override rules followed by a soft control-barrier-function filter): a classical
planner (frontier selection, A* routing, Voronoi partitioning with
consensus-based auction claims exchanged within a $3\,\si{m}$ radio range, and a
behavior tree), a random walk, and a MAPPO policy with a permutation-invariant
neighbor encoder and a centralized critic. In long single runs the classical
planner covered \textbf{100.0\%} of the sensed, non-obstacle area in the
warehouse, \textbf{98.2\%} in the office, \textbf{85.7\%} in the depot,
\textbf{100.0\%} in the cafe and \textbf{25.0\%} in the maze, which is a
property of those worlds and step budgets as much as of the controller. In a
robot-count sweep (one run per cell) coverage generally rose with team size,
and robots touched in 3 of 20 configurations. Trained MAPPO baselines
covered less than the classical planner in every world of an exploratory
single-episode matrix; a reward ablation with three training seeds per arm was
inconclusive. We also report three limits of the testbed itself: evaluation
outcomes vary between repeated runs of an identical checkpoint, the safety
margin is soft, and we did not test the architecture's claimed ability to
transfer to other team sizes. All data and code are archived with a DOI.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
multi-robot systems, area coverage, control barrier functions, MAPPO,

\section{Introduction}
%==============================================================================
Cooperative area coverage, in which a team of robots jointly sweeps an unknown
environment, underlies search, inspection and monitoring. Evaluating
coordination methods for it needs a simulator that models range sensing and
contact, and results that others can reproduce. MARS is a testbed built for
that purpose. It wraps Gazebo in a multi-agent environment, provides a
classical decentralized controller and a learned MAPPO baseline behind the
same safety stack, and archives per-run results.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A documented testbed with five worlds, three controllers and a common
        safety stack, described here as implemented (Sec.~\ref{sec:testbed}).
  \item Coverage ceilings of the classical controller in the five worlds, a
        robot-count sweep, and a comparison with trained MAPPO baselines, all
        from archived runs (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item An account of the testbed's limits: non-deterministic evaluation, a
        soft safety margin, and claims that remain untested
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}).
\end{itemize}
We make no claim that the learned policy is competitive, that the safety filter
gives a formal guarantee, or that the policy transfers to other team sizes.

%==============================================================================
\section{Related Work}
%==============================================================================
Frontier-based exploration~\cite{yamauchi1997frontier} and its multi-robot
extensions~\cite{burgard2005coordinated,amigoni2017multirobot} are the
classical baseline for this task; the consensus-based bundle
algorithm~\cite{choi2009cbaa} supplies the auction mechanism we use to resolve
duplicate target claims. MAPPO~\cite{yu2022mappo} is a strong baseline for
cooperative multi-agent learning; we train it with
RLlib~\cite{liang2018rllib}. The actor's neighbor encoder is a mean-pooled
embedding in the style of Deep Sets~\cite{zaheer2017deepsets}; it contains no
message passing, so we do not call it a graph neural network. Control barrier
functions~\cite{ames2019cbf} give a quadratic-program safety filter, and
barrier-function filters have been placed around learned
policies~\cite{cheng2019end}. We solve ours with OSQP~\cite{stellato2020osqp}
and add a slack variable, which makes it soft (Sec.~\ref{sec:safety}). A
companion study~\cite{pandhe2026mappo} examines the MAPPO baseline in depth.

\section{The Testbed}
\label{sec:testbed}
%==============================================================================
\subsection{Platform and Metrics}
Three TurtleBot3 Waffle robots (differential drive, 24-sector LiDAR, odometry)
run in Gazebo Harmonic under ROS\,2 Jazzy~\cite{macenski2022ros2,gazebo}. The
simulator is wrapped as a PettingZoo~\cite{terry2021pettingzoo} parallel
environment. The workspace is a grid of $0.4\,\si{m}$ cells. A cell is marked
visited when a robot's pose falls in it or a LiDAR ray (up to $2.0\,\si{m}$)
passes through it as free space. The \emph{area-coverage rate} is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ACR}=\frac{|\text{visited}\setminus\text{obstacle}|}
{N_{\text{cells}}-|\text{obstacle}|}\times100,
\end{equation}
where ``obstacle'' cells are those the robots have registered as obstacles, so
the denominator depends on what has been discovered. A \emph{wall contact} is
counted when the minimum LiDAR range is below $0.14\,\si{m}$ (the approximate
sensor-to-bumper distance); an \emph{agent contact} when two robots are closer
than $0.20\,\si{m}$ after the first 15 steps. The agent-contact threshold is
smaller than the filter's agent margin (Sec.~\ref{sec:safety}), so a zero count
does not show that the margin held.

\subsection{Observation and Action}
Each robot observes a 46-vector: 24 LiDAR sector ranges, goal distance and
bearing, linear and angular velocity, and nine neighbor slots of (distance,
bearing), padded with $(10.0,0.0)$. Actions are $(v,\omega)$ with
$|v|\le0.22\,\si{m/s}$ and $|\omega|\le1.0\,\si{rad/s}$. Because the vector has
a fixed length, the network accepts up to nine neighbors.

\subsection{Safety Stack}
\label{sec:safety}
Every controller's command passes through three stages.
\emph{(1) Override rules.} If the minimum LiDAR range over all beams is below
$0.22\,\si{m}$ and the commanded forward speed is positive, forward speed
becomes $-0.08\,\si{m/s}$; if another robot is within $0.45\,\si{m}$ and within
$72^\circ$ of the heading, forward speed becomes $-0.05\,\si{m/s}$.
\emph{(2) Soft CBF.} A quadratic program (OSQP) solves
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}\min_{v,\omega,\delta}\ &(v-v_{\text{nom}})^2+0.05(\omega-\omega_{\text{nom}})^2+P\delta^2\\ \text{s.t.}\ &A_kv+B_k\omega+\delta\ge-\gamma h_k,\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $0\le\delta\le10$, $P=1000$ and $\gamma=2.0$. Barrier terms come from LiDAR
returns within $0.6\,\si{m}$, evaluated at two lookahead points ($\pm0.12\,\si{m}$
on the heading axis) with obstacle radius $0.20\,\si{m}$, and from neighbors
within $1.2\,\si{m}$ with agent radius $0.45\,\si{m}$. The neighbor term treats
the neighbor's velocity as bounded and unmodeled. If the QP is infeasible the
filter returns $(\min(0,v_{\text{nom}}),\omega_{\text{nom}})$.
\emph{(3) Front safeguard.} Forward speed is set to $\min(0,v)$ if any of the
five forward-cone beams reads below $0.24\,\si{m}$.
Because of the slack $\delta$, the radii are soft constraints: we claim no
formal guarantee and report behaviour only.

\subsection{Classical Controller}
Each robot keeps its own visited and obstacle grids, built from its LiDAR.
When two robots are within $3.0\,\si{m}$ they merge their visited grids by
bitwise OR, a range-limited approximation of gossip consensus; a global grid is
kept only for monitoring and for computing ACR. Frontier cells are the nearest
unvisited candidates inside the robot's own Voronoi cell relative to sensed
neighbors, duplicate claims are resolved by a consensus-based auction among
peers in range, and a stale claim from a peer that has gone out of range expires after 30
steps. Routes use 8-connected A*
on an obstacle-inflated grid. A behavior tree selects, in priority order,
mission-complete standby or perimeter patrol, an escape maneuver
($v=-0.12\,\si{m/s}$, $\omega=\pm0.6\,\si{rad/s}$) when a robot has not moved
for 30 steps, frontier exploration, and a boundary-aware wander. Unreachable
targets are blacklisted. A correlative scan matcher corrects odometry drift.

\subsection{MAPPO Baseline}
A single policy is shared by all robots. Each neighbor's (distance, bearing)
pair is embedded by a two-layer ReLU network ($2\to16\to16$), masked and
mean-pooled, concatenated with the 28 ego features and passed through a
two-layer Tanh actor ($44\to64\to64$). A centralized critic ($76\to64\to64\to1$,
Tanh) takes the same 44 features plus a mean-pooled embedding
($48\to32\to32$) of the other agents' observation--action pairs. The
mean-pooled embedding is invariant to neighbor order and padding position, which
\texttt{tests/test\_permutation\_invariance.py} checks directly. Training uses
RLlib PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} with a single in-process rollout worker,
train batch 700, $90$-step episodes, minibatch 64, 5 epochs, learning rate
$10^{-4}$, clip 0.2, $\gamma=0.99$, GAE $\lambda=0.95$ and entropy
coefficient $0.01$. The per-step reward is
\begin{multline}
r_i=-0.01+5.0\,\Delta d_i+4.0\,n_{\text{new}}+20\,\mathbb{1}[\text{goal}]\\
-8.0\,\mathbb{1}[\text{wall}]-\rho_i,
\end{multline}
where $\Delta d_i$ is the reduction in goal distance, $n_{\text{new}}$ the number
of cells newly visited by any robot this step, and $\rho_i=0.5$ if another robot
is within $0.5\,\si{m}$ plus $10$ within $0.20\,\si{m}$. The goal is a random
point from a fixed per-world list of safe positions, reassigned on arrival; it
is less informed than the classical controller's map-aware frontier targets.
Evaluation uses greedy actions.

different outcomes (Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}), so single-episode numbers carry
unquantified run-to-run variance; we treat them as descriptive. Raw per-run
records are archived (Data availability).

\subsection{Learned versus Classical}
Table~\ref{tab:mx} compares the trained MAPPO policy (trained on the cafe,
zero-shot elsewhere) with the classical controller and a random walk, one
150-step episode per cell with three robots. The classical controller covered
more than MAPPO in all five worlds. This single-episode matrix is exploratory;
the companion study~\cite{pandhe2026mappo} reports a 10-episode cafe
comparison in which MAPPO's median coverage (14.5\%) was below both the
classical controller's (38.6\%) and a random walk's (29.6\%); the
per-episode data for that comparison are not archived.
Because the controllers differ in information (the classical one builds a map,
MAPPO sees only local ranges and a random goal), this compares an engineered
system with a minimally trained learned one, not two learned policies.

A reward ablation trained three MAPPO variants for 15 iterations with three
training seeds each. Averaged over seeds, coverage was 28.0\%, 31.0\% and 40.5\% for the baseline, a lower collision penalty and a higher
discovery bonus. Paired tests across the three seeds did not reach
$p<0.05$ for either variant, so we draw no conclusion about the rewards; see
the companion study for the analysis.

\subsection{Safety Observations}
Across the 20 sweep runs, the 25 matrix runs and a 27-episode ablation (72
runs), agent contacts were absent in 69 of 72, while coverage
ranged from 1.1\% to 98.6\% (across worlds and horizons), so the contact count did not distinguish weak
from strong controllers. In the office run the minimum inter-robot distance
was $0.38\,\si{m}$, below the $0.45\,\si{m}$ agent radius, with no contact,
which is the behaviour expected of a soft constraint. Two moving-hazard
scenarios (head-on at $0.38\,\si{m/s}$ and crossing at $0.22\,\si{m/s}$) were
run once each without contact; their raw traces are not archived.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
%==============================================================================
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Non-deterministic evaluation.} Three evaluations of one saved
        checkpoint with the same seed argument gave coverage between 24.6\%
        and 37.8\%, and contact counts of 0 to 19; the cause is simulator
        timing, since evaluation uses greedy actions. Single-episode cells
        (Tables~\ref{tab:sweep} and~\ref{tab:mx}) are descriptive only.
  \item \textbf{Soft safety margin; no formal guarantee.} See
        Sec.~\ref{sec:safety}. We did not measure how often or how much the
        override rules and filter change each controller's commands.
  \item \textbf{Transfer to other team sizes is untested.} The encoder accepts
        up to nine neighbors and is order-invariant, but no trained policy was
        evaluated with a different number of robots. The sweep uses the
        classical controller.
  \item \textbf{Ablations not run.} We did not ablate the CBF, the consensus
        merge, the behavior tree or the scan matcher, so we cannot say how
        much each contributes.
  \item \textbf{Scope.} Simulation only, one robot model, three robots in the
        learned experiments, one training world, short training (about
        $3\times10^4$ steps), and no comparison with other published
        coverage planners.
\end{itemize}

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%==============================================================================
MARS provides a documented, archived testbed for safety-filtered multi-robot
coverage. Its classical controller reached high coverage in all five worlds
given enough steps, coverage generally grew with team size, and a MAPPO
baseline underperformed it. The testbed's own limits matter as much as its
numbers: evaluation is not deterministic, the safety margin is soft, and
several architectural claims remain untested. We release the code, per-run
logs and scripts so that others can check and extend these results.

\paragraph*{Data availability.} Code, per-run results and scripts are in the
project repository, archived at \url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22758286}
(directory \url{checkpoints/}).
