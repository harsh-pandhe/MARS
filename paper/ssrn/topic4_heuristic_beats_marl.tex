\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Problem.} Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) is often
proposed as a route to learned coordination for multi-robot exploration
tasks where a classical decentralized planner is already a strong
baseline, but negative results -- where a MARL policy is trained under a
realistic budget and simply underperforms -- are rarely reported with the
same rigor as positive results.
\textbf{Methodology.} We train a centralized-critic MAPPO policy (Ray
RLlib) on a 3-robot area-coverage task in a simulated environment
(a furnished caf\'e, ROS~2/Gazebo) under fixed training budgets, and compare
it against a non-learned Frontier Heuristic controller (Voronoi
partitioning, A* routing, behavior-tree mission control) and a Random Walk
baseline, all filtered through an identical Control Barrier Function (CBF)
safety layer. We evaluate both in-distribution performance and zero-shot
transfer across four structurally different, unseen environments.
\textbf{Key findings.} Under a 10-episode-per-condition distributional
evaluation on the training environment (Protocol~A), the Frontier
Heuristic (38.6\% median area coverage rate, ACR) and Random Walk
(29.6\%) outperform the trained MAPPO policy (14.5\%, averaging 1.5\,m of
displacement per robot over 150 steps, a 2.7$\times$ heuristic advantage).
Under a separate, single-seed cross-environment matrix (Protocol~B,
150-step episodes, $N{=}3$), the Frontier Heuristic outperforms MAPPO in
every environment tested, by margins of 1.2$\times$ to 2.0$\times$
(achieving 57.0\% vs.\ 28.2\% in the caf\'e).
\textbf{Diagnosis and Ablation.} To test whether this low-displacement
behavior stems from a penalty-avoidance equilibrium induced by dense
negative reward signals, we conduct a three-arm, multi-seed reward
ablation (Protocol~C, 15 iterations, seeds 42, 7, 99): a Baseline
configuration (collision penalty $8.0$, discovery bonus $4.0$), a
LowPenalty configuration (collision penalty reduced to $2.0$), and a
HighDiscovery configuration (discovery bonus raised to $16.0$). While
reconciling an internal numerical inconsistency, we discovered that this
Gazebo-based evaluation harness is not fully deterministic even at a fixed
seed and a fixed saved checkpoint: three independent evaluation passes of
the identical LowPenalty configuration produced ACR values ranging from
$24.6\%$ to $37.8\%$ (pooled mean $31.0\% \pm 4.5$ over 9 episodes). Under
this pooled estimate, LowPenalty's apparent advantage over Baseline
($27.1\%$) does not clearly exceed measurement noise. HighDiscovery's
single-pass result ($41.2\%$, $+14.1$ points over Baseline) exceeds this
noise floor and is our best evidence that a reward manipulation helps, but
it was evaluated only once per seed and so carries the same class of
unquantified uncertainty. Notably, if real, HighDiscovery's gain is
\emph{not} explained by increased displacement (mean $14.4\,$m vs.\
Baseline's $15.3\,$m), complicating a purely penalty-avoidance account of
the original failure mode.
\textbf{Contribution.} We report this negative result, its reward
ablation, and the evaluation non-determinism we discovered while
attempting to finalize it, in full, including two independent
infrastructure faults resolved during training. We regard the
non-determinism finding as at least as important a contribution as the
ablation's substantive results: it means every single-episode-per-seed
number in this paper (including the five-world matrix, Table~\ref{tab:5world})
carries undisclosed uncertainty we did not have the resources to
fully quantify in this revision, and we report that limitation explicitly
rather than presenting point estimates as exact. We argue that an
evidence-scoped claim -- that MAPPO exhibited a reproducible
low-displacement failure mode, that a reward manipulation plausibly but
not certainly mitigates it, and that it did not close the gap to a strong
classical planner under the tested compute budgets -- is more defensible
and useful to practitioners than sweeping generalizations about MARL.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Multi-robot area coverage -- driving a team of robots to jointly maximize
explored floor area -- has a strong, well-understood non-learned baseline:
frontier-based exploration combined with a path planner and a
decentralized space-partitioning scheme. MARL is frequently proposed as a
way to learn a coordination policy for this class of task instead of
engineering one, typically evaluated against a classical baseline after
extensive training and tuning. This paper reports the opposite outcome
under a realistic, compute-constrained training budget, and treats getting
that negative result right -- rather than explaining it away -- as the
contribution.

We deliberately narrow the claim to what our evidence supports: this is a
report of a specific, reproducible failure mode of one MAPPO
configuration on one task family, not a general claim about MARL versus
classical planning. Section~\ref{sec:method} details the system, reward,
and three distinct evaluation protocols we ran (they use different sample
sizes and address different questions). Section~\ref{sec:results} reports
the results across all three protocols, including our reward ablation.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the mechanism, in-distribution
versus zero-shot transfer effects, and evidentiary limits.

\section{System and Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Platform and Safety Layer}
Three TurtleBot3 Waffle robots (differential drive, 2D LiDAR, IMU) are
simulated in Gazebo Harmonic under ROS~2 Jazzy \cite{macenski2022ros2, gazebo},
coordinated via a PettingZoo-compatible parallel multi-agent environment
\cite{terry2021pettingzoo}. Every commanded velocity, regardless of which
controller produced it, passes through an identical unicycle Control
Barrier Function (CBF) filter \cite{ames2019cbf}, solved as a quadratic
program via OSQP \cite{stellato2020osqp} at each control step, with separate safety
radii for static obstacles ($d_{\text{safe,obs}}=0.22$\,m) and neighboring
agents ($d_{\text{safe,agent}}=0.45$\,m). This isolates the comparison to
exploration policy rather than low-level collision-avoidance behaviour,
which is shared across all three controllers.

\subsection{Controllers Compared}
\textbf{Frontier Heuristic}: a non-learned controller combining frontier
cell selection, 8-connected grid A* \cite{hart1968astar} waypoint routing with
obstacle inflation, unreachable-target blacklisting to break wall-lock
deadlocks, and a \texttt{py\_trees} behavior tree for mission-level state
(explore, recover-from-stuck, patrol-on-saturation). This controller
encodes substantial task-specific structural knowledge (a discretized
map, a graph search algorithm, an explicit stuck-recovery policy) that a
learned policy must instead acquire implicitly from reward and
observations; we treat this asymmetry as part of the research question
(when is engineering that structure worthwhile versus learning it?)
rather than an oversight.

\noindent\textbf{Random Walk}: uniform random heading changes at fixed
intervals, CBF-filtered identically to the other two controllers.

\noindent\textbf{MAPPO}: a centralized-critic, shared-policy PPO
\cite{schulman2017ppo} implementation via Ray RLlib \cite{rllib}. Each
robot observes a 46-dimensional vector: 24 LiDAR range bins, its own pose
and velocity, and its assigned goal's relative position; the policy is a
2-layer MLP (256 units, ReLU) shared across agents, with a separate
centralized critic that additionally observes all agents' states. The
action space is continuous, $a_i=[v_i,\omega_i]$ (linear and angular
velocity), passed through the shared CBF filter described above. Training
used PPO clip 0.2, $\gamma=0.99$, GAE $\lambda=0.95$, learning rate
$10^{-4}$, entropy coefficient 0.01, train batch size 700, minibatch size
64, 5 SGD epochs per iteration, for 45 iterations (approximately 31{,}500
environment transitions in total). We report this total transition count
explicitly because ``45 iterations'' alone does not convey the sample
budget: this is on the order of $3\times10^4$ environment steps, at the
low end of what is typically reported for PPO-family algorithms on
continuous-control tasks, and we cannot rule out that additional training
would change the outcome (see Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Reward Function}
The per-robot, per-step reward is
\begin{equation}
r_t = -0.01 + 5.0\,\Delta d_{\text{goal}} + 4.0\,n_{\text{new-cells}} - 8.0\,\mathbb{1}[\text{wall collision}] - p_{\text{proximity}} + 20\,\mathbb{1}[\text{goal reached}]
\end{equation}
where $\Delta d_{\text{goal}}$ is the reduction in distance to the
robot's current goal since the previous step, $n_{\text{new-cells}}$ is
the number of previously-unvisited grid cells the swarm collectively
discovered this step, and $p_{\text{proximity}}$ is $0.5$ if any other
agent is within $0.5$\,m and an additional $10.0$ if within $0.20$\,m
(counted as a collision after the first 15 steps of an episode). The
per-cell discovery bonus was raised from an earlier value of 2.0 to 4.0
specifically because it was judged too small relative to the $\pm 20$
goal/collision terms to provide useful gradient signal toward the
coverage behaviour the policy is evaluated on; even after this change, a
single wall collision ($-8.0$) or close-proximity event ($-10.0$) is
substantially larger in magnitude than the reward for discovering several
new cells in a single step ($4.0$ each), which is the quantitative basis
for our hypothesis in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Area Coverage Rate (ACR)}
We define
\begin{equation}
\text{ACR} = \frac{N_{\text{visited reachable cells}}}{N_{\text{reachable cells}}}\times 100
\end{equation}
computed on a fixed-resolution ($0.4$\,m) occupancy grid per environment.
A cell is \emph{reachable} if it is not classified as a static obstacle
by the environment's known obstacle map; cells occupied only transiently
by a robot are not excluded from the denominator. A cell counts as
\emph{visited} the first time any robot's footprint overlaps it; repeat
visits do not increase ACR but are separately tracked as \emph{overlap
redundancy} (mean visits per visited cell), reported in
Figure~\ref{fig:boxplot}.

\subsection{Environments and Train/Test Split}
Five Gazebo worlds are used: \texttt{cafe} (furnished, $17\times8$\,m, 800
grid cells), \texttt{warehouse} (open logistics hall, $30\times50$\,m,
9{,}375 cells), \texttt{depot} (industrial, structural pillars and
boxsets, $29\times15$\,m, 3{,}000 cells), \texttt{office} (partitioned
floor plan, narrow doorways, $26.2\times18.3$\,m, 3{,}500 cells), and
\texttt{maze} (labyrinthine corridors, $64\times11$\,m, 4{,}800 cells, of
which only $\approx$17\% is physically reachable floor space).
\textbf{The MAPPO policy is trained exclusively on \texttt{cafe}.}
Evaluation on \texttt{warehouse}, \texttt{depot}, \texttt{office}, and
\texttt{maze} is therefore zero-shot transfer, not in-distribution
evaluation, and should be read as a generalization test rather than a
like-for-like comparison of learning capability per environment; we did
not train separate policies per environment, which we identify as the
most valuable follow-up experiment in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Three Evaluation Protocols}
We report results across three distinct evaluation protocols that address
different scientific questions and differ in sample size, replication,
and configuration:

\noindent\textbf{Protocol A (caf\'e deep-dive)}: 10 episodes per condition
(Random Walk, Frontier Heuristic, MAPPO nominal, MAPPO with injected
Gaussian LiDAR noise, MAPPO with single-agent failure), 150-step episodes,
in \texttt{cafe} only. This is the source of the 14.5\% (MAPPO) versus
38.6\% (heuristic) ACR and 1.5\,m displacement figures (a 2.7$\times$ ratio),
reported with full boxplot distributions in Figure~\ref{fig:boxplot}.

\noindent\textbf{Protocol B (five-world matrix)}: a single seed (42),
one episode per (world, controller) pair, 150-step episodes, across all
five environments and three controllers (plus two MAPPO perturbation
scenarios), reported in Table~\ref{tab:5world}. This protocol evaluates
cross-environment generalization breadth ($n{=}1$ per cell), showing that
the heuristic holds higher ACR across all five worlds (e.g., 57.0\% vs.\ 
28.2\% in \texttt{cafe}, a 2.0$\times$ margin).

\noindent\textbf{Protocol C (reward-coefficient ablation)}: controlled
15-iteration training budgets ($\approx 10{,}500$ transitions) across
three independent random seeds (42, 7, and 99), evaluating how sensitive
the policy's displacement and coverage are to the relative weight of the
collision penalty versus the discovery bonus, reported in
Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.

We treat Protocol~A's 2.7$\times$ margin (10-episode distributional
evaluation on an earlier checkpoint) and Protocol~B's 2.0$\times$ margin
(single-seed matrix) as two complementary evaluations rather than
interchangeable figures: Protocol~A isolates distributional stability in
the training domain, whereas Protocol~B demonstrates that the ranking
generalizes across diverse obstacle topologies. Protocol~C directly tests
the causal mechanism behind the policy's displacement.

\subsubsection{A Late-Discovered Limitation: Evaluation Non-Determinism}
\label{sec:nondeterminism}
While reconciling an apparent inconsistency between two sets of LowPenalty
numbers produced at different points in this project, we re-ran the
identical evaluation (same saved checkpoint file, same \texttt{--seed}
argument, same world, same episode length) a third time and obtained a
third, again different, result. Table~\ref{tab:nondeterminism} shows all
three passes.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\caption{Three independent evaluation passes of the identical LowPenalty checkpoint and seed arguments.}
\label{tab:nondeterminism}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Pass & Seed 42 ACR (\%) & Seed 7 ACR (\%) & Seed 99 ACR (\%) \\
\midrule
1 & 24.58 & 34.29 & 36.92 \\

3 & 26.69 & 29.90 & 28.08 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Pooling all 9 episodes gives a mean ACR of $30.96\% \pm 4.52$ (standard
deviation), i.e.\ a single-episode result can differ from the pooled mean
by nearly 7 percentage points in either direction. We attribute this to
real-time physics and ROS message timing jitter in the Gazebo-based
evaluation harness -- consistent with repeated \texttt{"Moved backwards in
time, re-publishing joint transforms"} warnings observed in these runs --
rather than to the fixed \texttt{--seed} argument, which controls
Gazebo's pseudo-random number generator but not wall-clock-dependent
message scheduling. \textbf{This means every single-episode-per-seed
number reported elsewhere in this paper, including the Protocol~B
five-world matrix (Table~\ref{tab:5world}) and the Baseline/HighDiscovery
rows of Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, carries this same undisclosed
uncertainty, which we did not identify until late in this revision.} We
did not have the resources remaining in this revision to re-run every
reported configuration multiple times; we disclose the issue explicitly
rather than silently retain single-episode point estimates as if they were
exact, and we treat this as the most important limitation of this paper's
quantitative claims. A rigorous follow-up should evaluate every
configuration across at least 5--10 repeated passes per seed (not just
distinct seeds) before treating any effect size in this ablation, or in
Table~\ref{tab:5world}'s per-world margins, as established.

Under this pooled, more honest LowPenalty estimate ($30.96\%$), its
apparent advantage over Baseline ($27.12\%$) shrinks to $+3.8$ points and
is no longer clearly distinguishable from the within-checkpoint noise
floor we just measured ($\pm 4.52$ points). We do not withdraw the
HighDiscovery result, whose single-pass effect ($+14.1$ points) is larger
than the LowPenalty noise floor and therefore more likely, though not
certain, to reflect a real effect rather than evaluation noise alone --
but we no longer present LowPenalty as a confirmed, consistent effect, and
revise the summary below accordingly.

The ablation results, read in light of this limitation, support the
following, more cautious conclusions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{HighDiscovery shows the larger, more likely-real effect;
    LowPenalty's effect is not distinguishable from measurement noise.}
    Relative to Baseline ($27.1\%$), the pooled LowPenalty estimate
    ($31.0\% \pm 4.5$) is within one standard deviation of Baseline and we
    do not treat it as a confirmed effect. HighDiscovery's single-pass
    estimate ($41.2\%$, $+14.1$ points) exceeds the measured noise floor
    and is our best evidence of a real, larger effect, though it was not
    itself repeated and so carries the same unquantified uncertainty in
    principle.
    \item \textbf{Where an effect is plausible, the displacement account is
    incomplete.} If HighDiscovery's gain is real, it is not explained by
    increased displacement: its mean displacement ($14.4\,$m) is not higher
    than Baseline's ($15.3\,$m). This would mean the coverage gain, if real,
    comes from more efficiently \emph{targeted} movement toward
    undiscovered cells rather than simply moving more -- a mechanism our
    original penalty-avoidance-only hypothesis did not anticipate.
    \item \textbf{Reward manipulations, if effective at all, help
    unevenly.} We are only able to say that strengthening the positive
    signal is a more promising lever than weakening the negative one under
    this budget, contingent on HighDiscovery's effect being real rather
    than noise; we can no longer claim LowPenalty reliably raises coverage,
    only that its pooled estimate is directionally higher than Baseline
    without clearing our own measured noise floor.
    \item \textbf{Variance is dominated by evaluation noise, not just seed
    choice.} The apparent seed-to-seed spread we originally reported
    (LowPenalty: $24.6\%$--$36.9\%$ across three \emph{distinct} seeds) is
    now known to be at least partly attributable to re-running the
    \emph{same} seed and checkpoint (Section~\ref{sec:nondeterminism}), not
    to genuine sensitivity to the seed argument itself. We do not have
    enough repeated passes to separate seed-driven variance from
    evaluation-noise variance for any configuration in this study.
    \item \textbf{Safety filter decoupling.} Inter-agent collisions remain
    strictly zero across all nine ablation runs (three configurations
    $\times$ three seeds), regardless of reward coefficients, demonstrating
    that the downstream CBF safety filter's guarantee is decoupled from
    policy competence or reward shaping, as argued in a companion paper on
    this system's safety layer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Mechanism: Empirical Validation and Evidentiary Bounds}
Our original hypothesis was that MAPPO's low-displacement failure mode
arises from a severe asymmetry between dense, immediate negative penalties
($-8.0$ for wall contact, $-10.0$ for agent proximity) and delayed,
incremental positive rewards ($4.0$ per newly discovered grid cell), such
that relieving the penalty would let the policy move more and thereby
cover more. Having discovered that our evaluation harness is not fully
deterministic even at fixed seed and checkpoint
(Section~\ref{sec:nondeterminism}), we can no longer treat LowPenalty's
single-pass coverage increase as confirmed evidence for this mechanism:
its pooled, 9-episode estimate ($31.0\% \pm 4.5$) does not clearly exceed
Baseline ($27.1\%$) once the measured noise floor is accounted for.

HighDiscovery's single-pass coverage gain ($+14.1$ points) is larger than
the measured noise floor and is therefore our best available evidence that
\emph{some} reward manipulation helps, but it was itself evaluated only
once per seed and so carries the same class of uncertainty in principle,
untested. What HighDiscovery's result does \emph{not} show, and what our
original hypothesis predicted it would, is a displacement increase: its
mean displacement ($14.4\,$m) is not higher than Baseline's ($15.3\,$m).
If the effect is real, it is more consistent with the positive discovery
signal directly rewarding useful, targeted movement than with the policy
simply becoming less risk-averse and moving more -- a mechanism distinct
from, and not anticipated by, our original penalty-avoidance-only
hypothesis. We hold this interpretation tentatively, contingent on the
effect surviving a properly repeated re-evaluation that this revision does
not include.

Even under the better-performing HighDiscovery configuration, MAPPO's mean
ACR ($41.2\%$) remains below the Frontier Heuristic's Protocol~B range
($38.6\%$--$57.0\%$) on comparable seeds, and we have not evaluated
HighDiscovery beyond a 15-iteration budget or beyond \texttt{cafe}. This
shows that reward tuning alone, at the budget and seed count tested here,
does not clearly close the gap:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structural knowledge advantage:} The Frontier Heuristic
    leverages an explicit occupancy grid, A* graph search, and Voronoi
    bidding to systematically dispatch robots to unexplored clusters. MAPPO,
    relying on local 24-bin LiDAR ranges and an MLP actor, must learn both
    mapping and coordinated pathfinding simultaneously from reward gradients.
    \item \textbf{Training budget limits:} At $3.15\times 10^4$ transitions
    (45 iterations) and $1.05\times 10^4$ transitions (15 iterations), our
    budget reflects realistic compute constraints for robotic simulation. We
    cannot rule out that asymptotic training ($>10^6$ transitions) with
    curriculum learning or reward normalization would eventually escape the
    local optimum.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{In-Distribution Performance vs.\ Zero-Shot Generalization}
A critical distinction highlighted by our protocols is in-distribution
learning versus cross-world transfer. MAPPO was trained exclusively in
\texttt{cafe}. Its performance in \texttt{warehouse}, \texttt{depot},
\texttt{office}, and \texttt{maze} represents zero-shot transfer across
drastically different floor plans (from an open $30\times 50$\,m hall to
a narrow labyrinth). The Frontier Heuristic maintains consistent advantages
across all topologies precisely because its graph-search and Voronoi
formulation is topology-agnostic, requiring zero retraining.

\section{Conclusion}
Under a fully specified, compute-bounded training protocol, MAPPO exhibited
a reproducible low-displacement failure mode on multi-robot area coverage.
Attempting to isolate its cause via a three-arm, multi-seed reward
ablation, we found that strengthening the discovery bonus produced the
larger, more likely-real coverage gain (mean ACR $27.1\%\to41.2\%$) via a
mechanism other than increased displacement -- revising our original
penalty-avoidance-only hypothesis -- while weakening the collision penalty
produced a smaller apparent gain (mean ACR $27.1\%\to31.9\%$ on a single
pass) that we subsequently found does not clearly exceed the measurement
noise of our own evaluation harness, a limitation we discovered only while
reconciling an internal inconsistency late in this revision and report in
full rather than concealing. Nonetheless, the classical Frontier Heuristic
retained superior performance across five diverse environments
($1.2\times$ to $2.0\times$ margin on single-pass estimates now known to
carry unquantified uncertainty) without requiring policy training. For
roboticists operating under bounded compute budgets, hand-engineered
structural coordination remains, on this evidence, a more sample-efficient
and more reliably measured choice than model-free MARL evaluated under a
single-episode-per-condition protocol; we recommend that any follow-up
work adopt repeated-episode evaluation per condition as a baseline
requirement, a methodological lesson this paper learned the hard way.

\appendix
\section{Infrastructure Faults Encountered During Training}
Two infrastructure faults were encountered and resolved during this work.
First, a segmentation fault inside \texttt{torch.optim.Adam.\_\_init\_\_}
under Ray actor threads was traced to a PyTorch TorchDynamo compilation
hook interacting with Ray's threading model; disabling TorchDynamo
(\texttt{TORCHDYNAMO\_DISABLE=1}) resolved it. Second, training halted at
iteration 34/45 when a host Wi-Fi disassociation event caused Ray's
distributed control plane (bound by default to the host's dynamic IP
rather than loopback) to lose connectivity to its own coordination
service; binding Ray explicitly to \texttt{127.0.0.1} and capping its
object store in-process resolved this and allowed training to resume and
complete to iteration 45. Neither fault altered the training
configuration, reward function, or evaluation protocol reported in the
main text; both are reported for reproducibility.
