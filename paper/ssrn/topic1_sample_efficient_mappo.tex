\begin{abstract}
\noindent
In our serial Gazebo setup, a single multi-robot training experiment is
limited to thousands rather than millions of environment steps. In an early run of a centralized-critic MAPPO policy for
three-robot area coverage ($15$ iterations, $4{,}500$ steps, RLlib default
GAE $\lambda=1.0$ and entropy coefficient $0.0$), we recorded policy entropy
falling to $-0.53$ and episode returns between $-227$ and $+3$, and
attributed this to the default hyperparameters. This paper is a reproducibility companion to a separate negative-result study~\cite{pandhe2026mappo}. We proposed a correction
(smaller $\lambda$, a nonzero entropy bonus, a larger batch, shorter episodes
and a rebalanced reward). The raw logs of that run were not kept, and the
attribution was never tested against a control. Here we test it: six
training runs treated as independent replicates, three per configuration, with per-iteration
metrics logged. We did not reproduce the collapse. Under a reconstruction of the original
configuration, entropy at iteration 15 was $1.75$, $1.50$ and $1.87$ (minimum
$0.79$ in one run, which then recovered), nowhere near $-0.53$. Under the
corrected configuration it was $1.95$, $2.12$ and $2.48$, higher in all
three runs (entropy rose by $0.36$ to $0.90$ over training, against
$-0.45$ to $+0.48$ for the reconstruction), but with three runs per arm the
smallest attainable two-sided permutation $p$ is $0.10$, which is also the
value obtained. Pooled per-episode return SDs were $72$ and $67$ (descriptive only), and mean returns were negative throughout and did not clearly improve in either arm. The
comparison changes five things at once and logs no task metric, so it
cannot say which change matters or whether the correction improves
coverage. We conclude that the original diagnosis, built from one
unreplicated run, is not supported, that run-to-run variability in entropy
is large enough for a single run to mislead, and that the corrected
configuration raises entropy modestly without our having shown a benefit.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} multi-agent reinforcement learning, MAPPO,
entropy collapse, environment-step budget, reproducibility, negative results

\section{Introduction}
Work on sample efficiency in deep reinforcement learning usually assumes that
environment steps are cheap or parallelizable. General-purpose robotics
simulators used for sensor-level fidelity (LiDAR ray casting, collision
geometry, ROS integration) are often run as a single real-time instance,
which, in our setup, makes a budget of thousands of environment steps per experiment a practical
ceiling rather than a choice. Under such budgets a batch contains only a few
complete episodes, and the usual intuitions about advantage-estimation
variance and exploration pressure are worth examining.

This paper began as a diagnosis. After one early training run showed what
looked like entropy collapse, we traced it, by reasoning about the
configuration, to RLlib's default $\lambda=1.0$ and entropy coefficient
$0.0$, proposed a correction, and wrote it up as a mechanistic case study. It
is now a report on testing that diagnosis. We ran the control we should have
run first, and the result does not support the original account. We report it
because the failure to reproduce is informative: it shows how a causal story
assembled from a single unreplicated run can look convincing and be wrong,
and it gives a three-run baseline for entropy trajectories under the reconstructed configuration.
The claim is narrow. We do not claim that default hyperparameters are safe in
small-batch multi-agent training in general, only that in this system, with
three runs per arm, we did not see the collapse we reported.

\section{Related Work}
Yu et al.~\cite{yu2022mappo} establish MAPPO with a centralized critic as a
strong baseline for cooperative multi-agent games; their results are
reported with far more environment steps than are available here. Work aimed
at closing the sample-budget gap includes differentiable surrogate models
alongside a non-differentiable simulator~\cite{asymmetricphysics2026} and
world-model-based learning without RL~\cite{agilemotor2024}, which require a
learned or differentiable dynamics model that we do not assume. Masked
reconstruction as an auxiliary task improves sample efficiency in
value-based MARL~\cite{maskedreconstruction2023}, and physics-informed
structure has been used for distributed multi-robot
learning~\cite{physicsinformedmarl2024}; both are complementary to the
hyperparameter-level question studied here. Henderson et
al.~\cite{henderson2018deep} show that results in deep RL depend heavily on
random seeds, implementation details and the number of runs, which is the
concern our replication illustrates. Colas et al.~\cite{colas2018many} and
Agarwal et al.~\cite{agarwal2021precipice} argue for power analysis and
interval estimates when few runs are available, and Andrychowicz et
al.~\cite{andrychowicz2021matters} show that on-policy implementation
choices (including advantage estimation and entropy regularization) can
change outcomes substantially; our small test is in that spirit but far
smaller in scale. A companion study~\cite{pandhe2026mappo}
evaluates the corrected configuration's downstream coverage performance
against a classical planner.

\section{System and the Original Configuration}
\label{sec:system}
The system is a shared MAPPO policy for three TurtleBot3 robots performing
cooperative area coverage in Gazebo Harmonic (ROS~2 Jazzy), trained with Ray
RLlib. Each robot observes a 46-dimensional vector: 24 LiDAR ranges, goal
distance and bearing, linear and angular velocity, and nine neighbor slots of
(distance, bearing). Each neighbor pair is embedded by a two-layer ReLU
network ($2\to16\to16$), masked and mean-pooled, concatenated with the 28 ego
features, and passed through a two-layer Tanh actor ($44\to64\to64$). A
centralized critic ($76\to64\to64\to1$, Tanh) takes the same 44 features plus
a mean-pooled embedding ($48\to32\to32$, ReLU) of the other agents'
observation--action pairs. Training runs with \texttt{num\_env\_runners}=0,
since Gazebo must run in the main process, so all rollouts are serial.
Learning rate $10^{-4}$, PPO clip $0.2$, $\gamma=0.99$, $5$ epochs and
minibatch size $64$ are common to all runs. Runs used code commit \texttt{1a83e72}, an AMD Ryzen~5 7600 (6 cores, 12 threads), about 30~GB RAM and Ubuntu 24.04.5 LTS. Wall-clock time was about $1{,}010$~s per original-arm run and $2{,}508$~s per corrected-arm run.

\textbf{Entropy metric and ``collapse.} The policy has two continuous action dimensions $(v,\omega)$. Training uses Ray RLlib 2.55.1 on the old (ModelV2) API stack with the PyTorch framework. For a box action space RLlib selects \texttt{TorchDiagGaussian}, whose entropy (per its source) is the per-dimension Gaussian entropy summed over the two action dimensions; PPO logs its mean over the training batch (all agents and timesteps in the batch, with the shared policy). It is the \emph{differential} entropy of the unsquashed Gaussian, before action clipping or normalization, so it can be negative and a negative value is not in itself pathological. We therefore use ``entropy collapse'' operationally to mean a sharp fall of logged entropy relative to the run's earlier values, not the sign of the value. Because the historical run lacks raw logs, we cannot retrospectively define a quantitative threshold; ``collapse'' is a descriptive label for the historical trajectory, not a formally thresholded outcome; the historical $-0.53$ is simply an unusually low observed value, with no demonstrated pathological threshold.

\textbf{Original observation.} The first configuration left GAE $\lambda$
and the entropy coefficient at RLlib's defaults ($1.0$ and $0.0$), used a
train batch of $300$ steps, $150$-step episodes, a collision penalty of
$-20$ and a coverage weight of $2.0$. In one $15$-iteration run ($4{,}500$
steps) we recorded final-iteration entropy of $-0.53$ and episode returns
between $-227$ and $+3$. These figures come from our notes of that run; its
logs were not archived, and we cannot re-derive them. Because the original code state is not archived, the arm called ``original below is a \emph{reconstruction} of the described settings, not the original run. The original account
attributed the entropy to two mechanisms: a $\lambda=1.0$ Monte-Carlo
estimator is high-variance when a batch holds only a few episodes, and a zero
entropy coefficient exerts no pressure against premature convergence.
Neither mechanism was tested. We also stated that a batch held ``three to
four'' episodes; in the runs reported below it held two.

\textbf{Proposed correction.} $\lambda=0.95$; entropy coefficient $0.01$;
train batch $700$ with $90$-step episodes (more distinct episodes per iteration at the same nominal iteration count; total steps and wall-clock time are reported below); collision penalty $-8$ and coverage weight $4.0$. We considered but
did not pursue curriculum learning and behavior-cloning warm starts.

\section{Controlled Test}
\label{sec:test}
\textbf{Questions and scope.} (1) Does the reconstructed original configuration reproduce the collapse? Mostly no. (2) Does the five-change correction raise entropy? Numerically yes, not established statistically. (3) Which change caused any difference? Unknown. (4) Does higher entropy give better coverage? Not tested here; the companion study~\cite{pandhe2026mappo} addresses downstream task performance, and this paper is restricted to the training-dynamics diagnosis.

\textbf{Design.} Two configurations, \emph{reconstructed original} and
\emph{corrected}, each trained for $15$ iterations, three runs treated as independent
per configuration, interleaved in time (original, corrected, original, \dots)
so that any drift in the simulator or host affects both. Everything not
listed in Section~\ref{sec:system} is identical, including code and world.
The Gazebo seed was fixed and RLlib's seed was not set, so runs differ
through RLlib's own randomness and simulator timing; they are not intended to be bitwise-reproducible, and the aim is to characterize variability under repeated launches. The experiment was resource-constrained and was intended to establish whether the historical behavior was readily reproducible, not to give a high-powered estimate of a treatment effect. Per-iteration policy entropy, losses, value-function explained
variance and episode returns were logged. The unit of independence is the
training run, so $n=3$ per arm. The analysis plan was fixed before we examined the outcomes of the six controlled runs: the primary outcome is entropy at iteration 15 (original lower
than corrected); the secondary outcome is the change in entropy from
iteration 1 to 15; return spread is descriptive only because return scales
differ between arms. One change was made after the first run finished: we
replaced the within-iteration range of returns, which grows with the number
of episodes (two against eight per iteration), with the standard deviation of
individual episode returns. Tests are exact two-sided permutation tests on the difference
of arm means. With three runs per arm there are $20$ permutations, so the
smallest attainable $p$ is $0.10$.

\textbf{What the comparison cannot separate.} The two configurations differ
in $\lambda$, entropy coefficient, batch size, episode length and reward
scale, and they collect different numbers of steps ($4{,}500$ against
$10{,}800$, because sampling ends on whole episodes; the nominal $15\times700$
is $10{,}500$). We therefore cannot attribute any difference to a single
change; the comparison tests a configuration-level intervention, not any individual hyperparameter mechanism. No coverage or other task metric was logged and the checkpoints were
not retained, so we say nothing here about task performance.

\subsection{Results}
\textbf{Entropy did not collapse in the reconstructed original.} Entropy at
iteration 15 was $1.75$, $1.50$ and $1.87$ (mean $1.71$). The lowest value
in any original run was $0.79$ (run 2, iteration 12), after which it rose to
$1.50$. No run approached the reported $-0.53$.

\textbf{The corrected configuration gave numerically higher entropy, not statistically distinguishable at $n=3$.}
Entropy at iteration 15 was $1.95$, $2.12$ and $2.48$ (mean $2.18$); the
difference in means is $-0.475$ (original minus corrected), exact $p=0.10$,
the smallest value attainable at $n=3$. The difference in means is $+0.48$ in favour of the corrected arm, with a descriptive, model-based $95\%$ Welch confidence interval of $[-0.08,\,1.03]$ (Welch--Satterthwaite $\mathrm{df}\approx3.6$); the interval is wide and includes zero, and it rests on normality assumptions that three runs cannot check. Because the corrected arm also received $2.4\times$ as many environment steps by iteration~15, any entropy difference cannot be separated from additional training exposure, let alone attributed to a single hyperparameter. Entropy rose over training in all
three corrected runs ($+0.36$, $+0.53$, $+0.90$) and was mixed in the
original runs ($+0.14$, $-0.45$, $+0.48$); the difference in the change is
$-0.54$, $p=0.20$. Mean entropy over the 15 iterations differed by only
$-0.16$ ($p=0.20$). Because the corrected configuration explicitly adds an entropy bonus, its higher logged entropy is partly an expected consequence of the modified objective rather than evidence of improved exploration or learning.

\textbf{Return spread (descriptive).} The standard deviation of per-episode
returns was $72$ (reconstructed original, pooled) against $67$ (corrected); run-level
values were $66$, $67$, $84$ and $61$, $69$, $71$. This is descriptive only and we do not treat it as confirmatory. Individual
episode returns reached about $-350$ in both arms (the historical note recorded $-227$ to $+3$, a different range from the one observed now), so large return swings also occurred in the corrected configuration and cannot be regarded as unique to the reconstructed defaults in these runs. Mean returns
did not improve with training. Comparing the last five iterations with the
first five, mean return was lower in four of the six runs, essentially
unchanged in one (original run 2, $-59\to-60$) and higher in one (corrected
run 3, $-61\to-51$). Averaged over all iterations the original runs gave
$-59$, $-62$ and $-66$ and the corrected runs $-43$, $-51$ and $-56$, but the
arms use different collision penalties and episode lengths, so we do not
compare these directly.

\section{Discussion}
The entropy-collapse diagnosis was not reproduced under a reconstructed version of the recorded original configuration. The mechanisms it
proposed remain plausible in principle, but the symptom they were meant to
explain, entropy collapse under the defaults, did not appear in three
attempts. Two explanations are consistent with this. The original run may
have been an unrepresentative draw: the original arm's entropy trajectories
are variable (final values $1.50$ to $1.87$, one transient dip to $0.79$),
so a single run can look like collapse or like nothing. Or the early run
differed from these in some respect we did not record, since the code
changed between them in ways not captured in the notes. We cannot tell these
apart, and we do not claim the first.

The corrected configuration raises entropy modestly and, in this sample,
consistently in direction, but the sample is too small to call it an
effect, and we did not measure whether it improves the task. The companion
study~\cite{pandhe2026mappo} trained the corrected configuration for $45$
iterations and found it did not close the gap to a classical planner, and
that two reward-coefficient variants of it did not produce a statistically
significant change in coverage at three seeds. Taken together, the
entropy correction is at most a stabilizing measure, not a demonstrated
route to a competent policy.

The practical point is about method. Entropy is cheap to log per iteration,
and three replicate runs per configuration cost a few hours here. Had we
done that before writing the diagnosis, we would not have published a causal
account of a pattern that does not replicate.

\section{What a Proper Follow-Up Would Require}
Change one factor at a time (e.g.\ $\lambda$ alone, entropy coefficient alone) at an equal environment-step budget; log a task metric such as area-coverage rate each iteration; set RLlib seeds explicitly; use at least five runs per arm; and retain checkpoints so that policies can be evaluated afterwards.

\section{Limitations}
Three runs per arm is small: the smallest attainable $p$ is $0.10$, so no
comparison here can reach conventional significance, and we report the
values instead. The comparison bundles five changes and cannot attribute an
effect to any. Fifteen iterations is short, and entropy might diverge later;
the original claim concerned iteration 15. We tested one environment and one
architecture. The simulator's timing is not deterministic, so the runs differ
from one another for reasons we do not control, which is part of what a
replicate measures. RLlib's seed was unset, and the two arms differ by $2.4\times$ in steps. We did not log a task metric or keep the checkpoints, so
we make no claim about coverage. The original run's logs are unavailable, so
the failure to reproduce it cannot be traced to a cause.

\section{Conclusion}
A diagnosis of entropy collapse from default RLlib hyperparameters,
formed from one unreplicated run, was not observed in three runs of a reconstructed
version of the recorded configuration: entropy at iteration 15 stayed between $1.50$ and
$1.87$. The corrected configuration raised entropy modestly in all three
runs without reaching significance at the attainable sample size, and we did
not measure a task benefit. We report the failure to reproduce, the
six-run entropy measurements and trajectories and the scripts that produced them, in the
hope that the baseline is more useful than the original diagnosis was.

\section{Infrastructure Faults}
Two distinct faults occurred during this
project and are easy to conflate. First, a native segmentation fault in
\texttt{torch.optim.Adam.\_\_init\_\_}; an earlier draft attributed it to a
local installation fault, but it was a TorchDynamo compilation hook failing
inside Ray's actor threads and was resolved by disabling TorchDynamo
(\texttt{TORCHDYNAMO\_DISABLE=1} together with the in-process setting).
Second, a later training run stopped at iteration 34 of 45 when a host Wi-Fi
interruption severed Ray's control plane, which had bound to the host's
dynamic address; binding Ray to the loopback interface and capping its
object store in-process resolved it. Neither affects the six runs here.
