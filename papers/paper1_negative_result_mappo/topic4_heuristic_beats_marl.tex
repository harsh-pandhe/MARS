\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Problem.} Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) is often
proposed as a route to learned coordination for multi-robot exploration
tasks where a classical decentralized planner is already a strong
baseline, but negative results -- where a MARL policy is trained under a
bounded compute budget and simply underperforms -- are rarely reported with the
same rigor as positive results.
\textbf{Methodology.} We train a centralized-critic MAPPO policy (Ray
RLlib) on a 3-robot area-coverage task in a simulated environment
(a furnished caf\'e, ROS~2/Gazebo) under fixed training budgets, and compare
it against a non-learned Frontier Heuristic controller (Voronoi
partitioning, A* routing, behavior-tree mission control) and a Random Walk
baseline, all filtered through an identical Control Barrier Function (CBF)
safety layer. We evaluate both in-distribution performance and zero-shot
transfer across four structurally different, unseen environments.
\textbf{Key findings.} Under a 10-episode-per-condition distributional
evaluation on the training environment (Protocol~A), the Frontier
Heuristic (38.6\% median area coverage rate, ACR) and Random Walk
(29.6\%) outperform the trained MAPPO policy (14.5\%, averaging 1.5\,m of
displacement per robot over 150 steps, a 2.7$\times$ heuristic advantage).
Under a separate, single-seed cross-environment matrix (Protocol~B,
150-step episodes, 3 robots), the Frontier Heuristic outperforms MAPPO in
every environment tested, by margins of 1.2$\times$ to 2.0$\times$
(achieving 57.0\% vs.\ 28.2\% in the caf\'e).
\textbf{Diagnosis and Ablation.} To test whether this low-displacement
behavior stems from a penalty-avoidance equilibrium induced by dense
negative reward signals, we conduct a three-arm reward ablation
(Protocol~C, 15 iterations): a Baseline configuration (collision penalty
$8.0$, discovery bonus $4.0$), a LowPenalty configuration (collision
penalty reduced to $2.0$), and a HighDiscovery configuration (discovery
bonus raised to $16.0$). While reconciling an internal numerical
inconsistency, we discovered that this Gazebo-based evaluation harness is
not fully deterministic even at a fixed seed and a fixed saved checkpoint,
and re-evaluated each trained checkpoint in three repeated evaluation passes
across the same three training seeds (42, 7, 99). \textbf{The correct unit of
statistical independence here is the trained checkpoint (one per seed),
not the individual evaluation episode}: our first attempt at this
analysis treated all 9 episodes per arm as independent samples, which is
pseudoreplication, since three of those nine measurements are repeated
evaluations of the identical trained policy. Correcting this, we compute
per-seed means (3 independent training replicates per arm) and a paired
$t$-test against Baseline across the 3 shared seeds. Under this corrected
analysis, \emph{neither} manipulation reaches conventional significance
at $n{=}3$: LowPenalty ($28.0\%\to31.0\%$ mean-of-seed-means, paired
$t{=}4.11$, $p{=}0.054$) is in fact numerically closer to significance,
with a smaller, more consistent per-seed effect, than HighDiscovery
($28.0\%\to40.5\%$, paired $t{=}2.93$, $p{=}0.10$), whose large mean gain
is driven disproportionately by 2 of 3 seeds (one seed showed almost no
gain over Baseline). We do not treat either result as confirmed, and we
report the earlier, statistically overstated $n{=}9$ analysis as an error
we caught and corrected rather than omitting it. Separately, a
coverage-efficiency metric (newly-discovered cells per meter travelled)
shows HighDiscovery at $21.3$ cells/m versus Baseline's $16.5$ and
LowPenalty's $16.5$ -- directionally consistent with HighDiscovery
improving movement \emph{efficiency} rather than raw displacement or ACR
alone; this descriptive metric is drawn from the same three seeds as the
significance test and should be read as suggestive, not as an
independent confirmation.
\textbf{Contribution.} We report this negative result, its reward
ablation, the evaluation non-determinism we discovered, and a
pseudoreplication error in our own initial statistical analysis that we
caught and corrected before treating it as a real finding, in full,
including two independent infrastructure faults resolved during training.
We regard the non-determinism finding and the pseudoreplication
correction as at least as important a contribution as the ablation's
substantive results: they mean every single-episode-per-seed number in
this paper (including the five-world matrix, Table~\ref{tab:5world}, still
$n{=}1$ per cell and explicitly exploratory rather than confirmatory) and
every apparent effect size must be read with real statistical caution, and
we report that limitation explicitly rather than presenting point
estimates or underpowered significance tests as settled fact. We argue
that an evidence-scoped claim -- that MAPPO consistently exhibited a
low-displacement behavioral failure mode, that a reward manipulation
plausibly mitigates it, but this effect is not statistically confirmed at the
independent-replicate sample sizes we could afford, and that it did not
close the gap to a strong classical planner under the tested compute
budgets -- is more defensible and useful to practitioners than sweeping
generalizations about MARL.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Multi-robot area coverage -- driving a team of robots to jointly maximize
explored floor area -- has a strong, well-understood non-learned baseline:
frontier-based exploration \cite{yamauchi1997frontier, burgard2005coordinated}
combined with a path planner and a decentralized space-partitioning scheme
\cite{amigoni2017multirobot}. Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)
\cite{lowe2017maddpg, yu2022mappo} is frequently proposed as a way to learn a
coordination policy for this class of task instead of engineering one,
typically evaluated against a classical baseline after extensive training and
tuning. This paper reports the opposite outcome under a modest,
compute-constrained training budget (we avoid the word ``realistic,'' since
we make no claim about what budget is representative of the field; ours is
simply what we could afford, and we cannot rule out that the same
architecture would behave differently at 5--10$\times$ the sample budget used
here), and treats getting that negative result right -- including correcting a
statistical error we made along the way \cite{henderson2018deep} -- rather
than explaining it away, as the contribution.

We deliberately narrow the claim to what our evidence supports: this is a
report of a specific, consistently-observed behavioral failure mode of
one MAPPO configuration on one task family, not a general claim about MARL versus
classical planning. Section~\ref{sec:method} details the system, reward,
and three distinct evaluation protocols we ran (they use different sample
sizes and address different questions). Section~\ref{sec:results} reports
the results across all three protocols, including our reward ablation.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the mechanism, in-distribution
versus zero-shot transfer effects, and evidentiary limits.

\section{System and Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Platform and Safety Layer}
Three TurtleBot3 Waffle robots (differential drive, 2D LiDAR, IMU) are
simulated in Gazebo Harmonic under ROS~2 Jazzy \cite{macenski2022ros2, gazebo},
coordinated via a PettingZoo-compatible parallel multi-agent environment
\cite{terry2021pettingzoo}. Every commanded velocity, regardless of which
controller produced it, passes through an identical unicycle Control
Barrier Function (CBF) filter \cite{ames2019cbf}, solved as a quadratic
program via OSQP \cite{stellato2020osqp} at each control step, with separate safety
radii for static obstacles ($d_{\text{safe,obs}}=0.22$\,m) and neighboring
agents ($d_{\text{safe,agent}}=0.45$\,m). All controllers share this
identical CBF filter, controlling for the low-level collision-avoidance
implementation itself; however, we did not measure how often or by how
much the filter modifies each controller's requested action before
execution, so we cannot rule out that the filter mediates the observed
policy behaviour differently across controllers (e.g.\ if MAPPO requests
unsafe actions far more often than the heuristic, the filter's
interventions -- not just the underlying policy -- could shape the
displacement patterns we report).

\subsection{Controllers Compared}
\textbf{Frontier Heuristic}: a non-learned controller combining frontier
cell selection, 8-connected grid A* \cite{hart1968astar} waypoint routing with
obstacle inflation, unreachable-target blacklisting to break wall-lock
deadlocks, and a \texttt{py\_trees} behavior tree for mission-level state
(explore, recover-from-stuck, patrol-on-saturation). This controller
encodes substantial task-specific structural knowledge (a discretized
map, a graph search algorithm, an explicit stuck-recovery policy) that a
learned policy must instead acquire implicitly from reward and
observations; we treat this asymmetry as part of the research question
(when is engineering that structure worthwhile versus learning it?)
rather than an oversight.

\noindent\textbf{Random Walk}: uniform random heading changes at fixed
intervals, CBF-filtered identically to the other two controllers.

\noindent\textbf{MAPPO}: a centralized-critic, shared-policy implementation of Multi-Agent PPO \cite{yu2022mappo, schulman2017ppo} via Ray RLlib \cite{rllib}. Each
robot observes a 46-dimensional vector:
\[
o_i = [\ell_{1:24},\; d_{\text{goal}},\; \theta_{\text{goal}},\; v,\; \omega,\; n_{1:9,1:2}]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{46}
\]
consisting of 24 LiDAR range bins
($\ell$), relative distance and bearing to the robot's current goal
waypoint ($d_{\text{goal}}, \theta_{\text{goal}}$), linear and angular
velocity ($v, \omega$), and 9 neighbor slots $\times$ 2 features each
($n$, padded with a sentinel value of $10.0$ range at $0.0$ bearing when
fewer than 9 neighbors are present). The goal waypoint itself is drawn
uniformly at random, on episode start and again each time it is reached,
from a fixed per-world list of pre-validated safe points
(\texttt{self.safe\_goals}); this same environment-level mechanism
supplies the goal signal for \emph{all} controllers uniformly, including
Random Walk, but it is distinct from -- and considerably less informed
than -- the Frontier Heuristic's own frontier-cell target selection
(Section~\ref{sec:method}, above), which reasons over the discretized
occupancy map rather than a fixed random waypoint list. This is an
important asymmetry: MAPPO's ``goal'' observation is not the same
quality of guidance signal the heuristic constructs for itself, and part
of the heuristic's advantage may be attributable to this richer,
map-aware target selection rather than to the exploration policy alone.
Table~\ref{tab:controller-assumptions} summarizes these structural
and informational differences across all three evaluated controllers.
The policy is a 2-layer MLP (256 units, ReLU) shared across agents, with a
separate centralized critic that additionally observes all agents'
states. The action space is continuous, $a_i=[v_i,\omega_i]$ (linear and
angular velocity), passed through the shared CBF filter described above,
identically for every controller; we did not measure how often or by how
much the CBF filter modifies MAPPO's requested actions relative to the
other controllers, which we identify as a natural follow-up diagnostic.
Training
used PPO clip 0.2, $\gamma=0.99$, GAE $\lambda=0.95$, learning rate
$10^{-4}$, entropy coefficient 0.01, train batch size 700, minibatch size
64, 5 SGD epochs per iteration, for 45 iterations (approximately 31{,}500
environment transitions in total). We report this total transition count
explicitly because ``45 iterations'' alone does not convey the sample
budget: this is on the order of $3\times10^4$ environment steps, at the
low end of what is typically reported for PPO-family algorithms on
continuous-control tasks, and we cannot rule out that additional training
would change the outcome (see Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Reward Function}
The per-robot, per-step reward is
\begin{equation}
r_t = -0.01 + 5.0\,\Delta d_{\text{goal}} + 4.0\,n_{\text{new-cells}} - 8.0\,\mathds{1}[\text{wall collision}] - p_{\text{proximity}} + 20\,\mathds{1}[\text{goal reached}]
\end{equation}
where $\Delta d_{\text{goal}} = d_{\text{goal}, t-1} - d_{\text{goal}, t}$ is the reduction in distance to the
robot's current goal since the previous step, $n_{\text{new-cells}}$ is
the number of previously-unvisited grid cells the swarm collectively
discovered this step, and $p_{\text{proximity}}$ is $0.5$ if any other
agent is within $0.5$\,m and an additional $10.0$ if within $0.20$\,m
(counted as a collision after the first 15 steps of an episode). The
per-cell discovery bonus was raised from an earlier value of 2.0 to 4.0
specifically because it was judged too small relative to the $\pm 20$
goal/collision terms to provide useful gradient signal toward the
coverage behaviour the policy is evaluated on; even after this change, a
single wall collision ($-8.0$) or close-proximity event ($-10.0$) is
substantially larger in magnitude than the reward for discovering several
new cells in a single step ($4.0$ each), which is the quantitative basis
for our hypothesis in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Area Coverage Rate (ACR)}
We define
\begin{equation}
\text{ACR} = \frac{N_{\text{visited reachable cells}}}{N_{\text{reachable cells}}}\times 100
\end{equation}
computed on a fixed-resolution ($0.4$\,m) occupancy grid per environment.
A cell is \emph{reachable} if it is not classified as a static obstacle
by the environment's known obstacle map; cells occupied only transiently
by a robot are not excluded from the denominator. A cell counts as
\emph{visited} the first time any robot's footprint overlaps it; repeat
visits do not increase ACR but are separately tracked as \emph{overlap
redundancy} (mean visits per visited cell), reported in
Figure~\ref{fig:boxplot}.

\subsection{Environments and Train/Test Split}
Five Gazebo worlds are used: \texttt{cafe} (furnished, $17\times8$\,m, 800
grid cells), \texttt{warehouse} (open logistics hall, $30\times50$\,m,
9{,}375 cells), \texttt{depot} (industrial, structural pillars and
boxsets, $29\times15$\,m, 3{,}000 cells), \texttt{office} (partitioned
floor plan, narrow doorways, $26.2\times18.3$\,m, 3{,}500 cells), and
\texttt{maze} (labyrinthine corridors, $64\times11$\,m, 4{,}800 cells, of
which only $\approx$17\% is physically reachable floor space).
\textbf{The MAPPO policy is trained exclusively on \texttt{cafe}.}
Evaluation on \texttt{warehouse}, \texttt{depot}, \texttt{office}, and
\texttt{maze} is therefore zero-shot transfer, not in-distribution
evaluation, and should be read as a generalization test rather than a
like-for-like comparison of learning capability per environment; we did
not train separate policies per environment, which we identify as the
most valuable follow-up experiment in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Three Evaluation Protocols}
We report results across three distinct evaluation protocols that address
different scientific questions and differ in sample size, replication,
and configuration:

\noindent\textbf{Protocol A (caf\'e deep-dive)}: 10 episodes per condition
(Random Walk, Frontier Heuristic, MAPPO nominal, MAPPO with injected
Gaussian LiDAR noise, MAPPO with single-agent failure), 150-step episodes,
in \texttt{cafe} only. This is the source of the 14.5\% (MAPPO) versus
38.6\% (heuristic) ACR and 1.5\,m displacement figures (a 2.7$\times$ ratio),
reported with full boxplot distributions in Figure~\ref{fig:boxplot}.

\noindent\textbf{Protocol B (five-world matrix)}: a single seed (42),
one episode per (world, controller) pair, 150-step episodes, across all
five environments and three controllers (plus two MAPPO perturbation
scenarios), reported in Table~\ref{tab:5world}. This protocol evaluates
cross-environment generalization breadth ($n{=}1$ per cell), showing that
the heuristic holds higher ACR across all five worlds (e.g., 57.0\% vs.\ 
28.2\% in \texttt{cafe}, a 2.0$\times$ margin).

\noindent\textbf{Protocol C (reward-coefficient ablation)}: controlled
15-iteration training budgets ($\approx 10{,}500$ transitions) across
three independent random seeds (42, 7, and 99), evaluating how sensitive
the policy's displacement and coverage are to the relative weight of the
collision penalty versus the discovery bonus, reported in
Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.

We treat Protocol~A's 2.7$\times$ margin (10-episode distributional
evaluation on an earlier checkpoint) and Protocol~B's 2.0$\times$ margin
(single-seed matrix) as two complementary evaluations rather than
interchangeable figures: Protocol~A isolates distributional stability in
the training domain, whereas Protocol~B (exploratory, $n{=}1$ per cell)
suggests the ranking may generalize across diverse obstacle topologies,
without statistically confirming it. Protocol~C is our only controlled
causal probe of the mechanism behind the policy's displacement, and even
there neither ablation arm reached significance at the achievable sample
size (Section~\ref{sec:corrected-stats}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Protocol B: Exploratory Five-World Comparison (Not a Statistical Result)}
Table~\ref{tab:5world} reports ACR and total swarm displacement from a
single-seed, single-episode-per-cell, five-world matrix. Given the
per-checkpoint evaluation noise we later measured directly in Protocol~C
(SD up to $\pm 8.4$ points on a single arm, Section~\ref{sec:ablation}),
an $n{=}1$-per-cell table cannot support a statistical claim, and several
of the smaller margins below (the $1.2$--$1.3\times$ rows) are plausibly
within that noise band. We present this table as an \textbf{exploratory}
indication that the heuristic's advantage is not confined to one
environment, not as confirmatory evidence with a defensible effect size;
we did not have the resources in this revision to repeat all 25
(world $\times$ controller) cells multiple times each, which would be
required to make this table statistically comparable to Protocol~C.

\subsection{Protocol A: Caf\'e Distributional Comparison}
Figure~\ref{fig:boxplot} shows the 10-episode-per-condition distribution

\subsection{Protocol C: Multi-Seed, Three-Arm Reward-Coefficient Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}
To empirically test whether the policy's exploration failure is caused by
the dense collision penalty term ($-8.0$) overwhelming the discovery bonus
($4.0$), we performed a reward ablation study under a controlled 15-iteration
budget ($\approx 10{,}500$ transitions) with three configurations, each
trained from scratch under identical hyperparameters: \textbf{Baseline}
(collision penalty $8.0$, discovery bonus $4.0$, i.e.\ the coefficients
used everywhere else in this paper), \textbf{LowPenalty} (collision
penalty reduced to $2.0$, discovery bonus held at $4.0$), and
\textbf{HighDiscovery} (collision penalty held at $8.0$, discovery bonus
raised to $16.0$). After discovering that a single evaluation pass is not
representative of a checkpoint's behaviour
(Section~\ref{sec:nondeterminism}), we evaluated \emph{every} arm's
trained checkpoint in three repeated evaluation passes across the same three
seeds (42, 7, 99), giving 9 episodes per configuration and 27 episodes in
total -- equal evaluation depth across all three arms, correcting the
single-pass Baseline/HighDiscovery numbers reported in an earlier
revision of this paper (this is a repeated-measurement count, not an
independent-sample count; see Section~\ref{sec:corrected-stats} for the
statistically correct unit of independence). Table~\ref{tab:ablation}
summarizes the pooled results; all values are taken directly from
per-episode evaluation logs with no post-hoc
adjustment, and are persisted in this project's public results directory
for independent verification.

\subsubsection{A Late-Discovered Limitation: Evaluation Non-Determinism}
\label{sec:nondeterminism}
While reconciling an apparent inconsistency between two sets of LowPenalty
numbers produced at different points in this project, we re-ran the
identical evaluation (same saved checkpoint file, same \texttt{--seed}
argument, same world, same episode length) a third time and obtained a
third, again different, result. Table~\ref{tab:nondeterminism} shows all
three passes.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\caption{Three independent evaluation passes of the identical LowPenalty checkpoint and seed arguments.}
\label{tab:nondeterminism}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Pass & Seed 42 ACR (\%) & Seed 7 ACR (\%) & Seed 99 ACR (\%) \\
\midrule
1 & 24.58 & 34.29 & 36.92 \\
2 & 37.84 & 30.11 & 30.20 \\
3 & 26.69 & 29.90 & 28.08 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Pooling all 9 episodes gives a mean ACR of $30.96\% \pm 4.52$ (standard
deviation), i.e.\ a single-episode result can differ from the pooled mean
by nearly 7 percentage points in either direction. We verified that all
evaluation rollouts in this paper, across every protocol, use greedy
(non-stochastic) action selection at evaluation time
(RLlib's \texttt{compute\_actions(..., explore=False)}, in
\texttt{evaluate\_benchmarks.py}, called uniformly by every evaluation
entry point including the ablation harness); the policy's action output
is therefore deterministic given an observation, which rules out
stochastic action sampling as the source of the variance we measured. The
observed nondeterminism is consistent with real-time physics and ROS
message-timing effects in the Gazebo-based evaluation harness -- alongside
repeated warnings (\texttt{"Moved backwards in time,}
\texttt{re-publishing joint transforms"}) observed in these runs -- rather
than the fixed \texttt{--seed} argument, which controls Gazebo's
pseudo-random number generator but not wall-clock-dependent message
scheduling or physics-thread race conditions \cite{engstrom2020implementation,
henderson2018deep}. While we
have not experimentally isolated the exact variance decomposition between
physics stepping and message queuing, these observations confirm that
deterministic evaluation in continuous multi-robot simulation requires
strict lockstep physics rather than seed control alone.
\textbf{This means every single-episode-per-seed
number reported elsewhere in this paper, particularly the Protocol~B
five-world matrix (Table~\ref{tab:5world}), carries this same undisclosed
uncertainty.} In response, we re-evaluated the Baseline and HighDiscovery
arms of the ablation three times each as well (matching LowPenalty's
treatment), giving equal, 9-episode statistical footing across all three
arms (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}); we did not have the resources in this
revision to similarly repeat the five-world Protocol~B matrix, and that
table's margins should still be read as indicative rather than
statistically established.

With all three ablation arms now evaluated three times each, our first
analysis treated all 9 episodes per arm as independent samples and
computed Welch's $t$-test against Baseline, obtaining $t=4.04$ for
HighDiscovery and $t=1.48$ for LowPenalty. \textbf{This analysis is
incorrect, and we report the error rather than silently fixing it and
moving on.} Three of each arm's nine episodes are repeated evaluations of
the \emph{identical trained checkpoint} for a given seed -- they are not
independent training replicates, and treating them as nine independent
samples is pseudoreplication: it inflates the effective sample size and
therefore understates the true uncertainty in the estimated effect.

\subsubsection{Corrected Analysis: Per-Seed Means, $n=3$}
\label{sec:corrected-stats}
The correct independent experimental unit in this design is the
\emph{trained checkpoint}, one per seed, of which we have exactly three
per arm. We therefore aggregate each seed's three repeated evaluations
into a single per-seed mean, and compare arms using a paired $t$-test
across the three shared seeds (Table~\ref{tab:paired}).

With $n=3$ independent seeds, paired $t$-tests give: LowPenalty vs.\
Baseline, $t=4.11$, $p=0.054$; HighDiscovery vs.\ Baseline, $t=2.93$,
$p=0.10$ (both two-tailed, $df=2$). \textbf{Neither reaches the
conventional $\alpha=0.05$ threshold.} More strikingly, this corrected
analysis \emph{reverses} the qualitative ranking from our initial,
flawed $n=9$ analysis: LowPenalty, not HighDiscovery, is numerically
closer to significance, because its per-seed effect is small but
consistent across all three seeds (SD of paired differences $1.24$),
whereas HighDiscovery's much larger mean effect is driven
disproportionately by two of three seeds (seed 99's gain, $+4.35$ points,
is an order of magnitude smaller than seeds 42 and 7's), giving it far
higher between-seed variance (SD $7.38$) despite the larger point
estimate. We regard this reversal as the paper's clearest illustration of
why the choice of statistical unit matters more than the choice of test:
had we not caught the pseudoreplication error, we would have published
the opposite qualitative conclusion about which reward manipulation is
more trustworthy.

As a descriptive complement to the inferential test above -- not a
substitute for it, and computed from the same three seeds and therefore
still subject to the same evaluation non-determinism -- we computed a
coverage efficiency metric (newly-discovered grid cells per meter of
swarm displacement, using \texttt{cafe}'s $800$-cell grid) for all three
arms: Baseline $16.48$ cells/m (SD $2.22$), LowPenalty $16.54$ cells/m (SD
$1.91$, indistinguishable from Baseline), and HighDiscovery $21.29$
cells/m (SD $2.09$). This is directionally consistent with HighDiscovery
improving the \emph{efficiency} of movement toward useful cells rather
than the \emph{quantity} of movement (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}); we
report it as descriptive, suggestive evidence for the mechanism, not as
an independently-powered confirmation of it.

The ablation results, re-analyzed correctly, support the following,
considerably more cautious conclusions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Neither reward manipulation reaches statistical
    significance at the number of independent training replicates we
    could afford ($n=3$ seeds).} LowPenalty ($p=0.054$) and HighDiscovery
    ($p=0.10$) are both plausible but unconfirmed. We explicitly retract
    the earlier claim, based on a pseudoreplicated analysis, that
    HighDiscovery was ``statistically supported'' while LowPenalty was
    not; the corrected analysis suggests, if anything, the opposite
    ordering by test statistic, though neither is conclusive.
    \item \textbf{Coverage efficiency, not raw displacement, best
    distinguishes HighDiscovery from the other two arms.} HighDiscovery's
    cells-per-meter efficiency ($21.3$) is numerically higher than both
    Baseline's and LowPenalty's (both $\approx 16.5$), while its raw mean
    displacement ($15.27\,$m) does not substantially exceed Baseline's
    ($13.69\,$m). This is our best available evidence that reward shaping
    here alters \emph{how} the policy moves, not merely \emph{how much},
    though we caution this is a within-arm descriptive statistic, not an
    independently significance-tested effect.
    \item \textbf{Substantial evaluation noise was observed alongside
    seed-to-seed variation.} The seed-to-seed spread we originally
    reported for LowPenalty (24.6\%--36.9\% across three \emph{distinct}
    seeds, single pass each) turned out to be substantially inflated by
    re-running the \emph{same} seed and checkpoint
    (Section~\ref{sec:nondeterminism}), not solely by genuine sensitivity
    to the seed argument. We do not, however, have a formal
    variance-components estimate: with only three training seeds we
    cannot confidently establish that evaluation noise \emph{exceeds}
    seed-driven variance in general, only that both are present and
    non-negligible. The per-seed means in Table~\ref{tab:paired} are our
    best attempt at separating the two, but three training seeds is not
    enough to fully characterize either.
    \item \textbf{Collision variance is not confined to ACR.} Baseline
    showed 0 wall contacts in 6 of 9 episodes but 6, 19, and 11 contacts
    in the remaining 3 (all from the same repeated-evaluation pass),
    indicating the non-determinism we discovered affects collision counts,
    not only coverage. Agent-agent collisions, however, remained at zero
    across all 27 ablation episodes regardless of reward coefficients or
    evaluation pass.
    \item \textbf{No inter-agent collisions observed.} Zero inter-agent
    collisions occurred across all 27 ablation episodes (three
    configurations $\times$ 9 episodes each), regardless of reward
    coefficients. Twenty-seven successful trials do not constitute a
    formal proof of the CBF filter's safety guarantee -- that guarantee
    follows from the filter's QP formulation, not from empirical trial
    counts -- but
    this observation is consistent with the filter's intended behaviour
    remaining unaffected by policy competence or reward shaping, as argued
    on formal grounds in a companion paper on this system's safety layer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Mechanism: Empirical Validation and Evidentiary Bounds}
Our original hypothesis was that MAPPO's low-displacement failure mode
arises from a severe asymmetry between dense, immediate negative penalties
($-8.0$ for wall contact, $-10.0$ for agent proximity) and delayed,
incremental positive rewards ($4.0$ per newly discovered grid cell), such
that relieving the penalty would let the policy move more and thereby
cover more. Having discovered both that our evaluation harness is not
fully deterministic at fixed seed and checkpoint
(Section~\ref{sec:nondeterminism}) and that our first attempt to correct
for this via a 9-episode Welch's-$t$ analysis was itself statistically
invalid (pseudoreplication; Section~\ref{sec:corrected-stats}), the
corrected $n=3$ paired analysis shows that neither reward manipulation
reached statistical significance at the three independent training seeds we
could afford (LowPenalty: paired $t=4.11, p=0.054$; HighDiscovery: paired
$t=2.93, p=0.10$). We explicitly caution against interpreting $p=0.054$ as
marginal support or ``near significance'': three independent seeds
provide insufficient statistical power to distinguish true policy effects
from seed variance in continuous multi-robot domains \cite{henderson2018deep},
and we cannot claim confirmed support for the original penalty-avoidance
hypothesis from either arm.

We can, however, offer a more careful reading of what the data are
\emph{consistent with}. HighDiscovery's point estimate is the larger of
the two ($28.0\%\to40.5\%$ vs.\ LowPenalty's $28.0\%\to31.0\%$), and its
coverage-efficiency metric (cells discovered per meter travelled, $21.3$
vs.\ Baseline's $16.5$) is the one descriptive quantity in this ablation
that shows the largest separation from Baseline, though it too is drawn
from the same three seeds and is not itself significance-tested. Taken
together with the observation that HighDiscovery's mean displacement
($15.3\,$m) is \emph{not} substantially higher than Baseline's
($13.7\,$m), the most defensible reading is: \emph{if} strengthening the
discovery signal has a real effect, it is more consistent with the policy
moving more \emph{purposefully} toward undiscovered cells than with it
simply becoming less risk-averse and moving more in general. We list this
as our leading hypothesis rather than a confirmed mechanism, alongside
three competing explanations we cannot yet rule out with the data
collected: (i) the larger discovery bonus may simply improve credit
assignment by making an already-sparse reward signal proportionally less
sparse relative to the fixed step and collision penalties, independent of
any change in \emph{targeting} behavior; (ii) the reward-coefficient
change may shift the effective exploration-exploitation balance under a
fixed entropy coefficient, altering which regions of state space the
policy visits during the (short) training run rather than altering
learned targeting behavior at evaluation time; and (iii) the effect may
be substantially driven by 2 of the 3 training seeds
(Table~\ref{tab:paired}), in which case it may reflect a property of
those two particular training runs rather than a general property of the
reward coefficient. Distinguishing these requires either many more
training seeds, or direct diagnostics (e.g.\ the CBF filter's
intervention rate and magnitude, and the spatial distribution of visited
cells relative to the frontier) that we did not collect in this revision
and identify as the most valuable follow-up measurement.

HighDiscovery reaches a mean ACR of $40.5\%$ under the 15-iteration
ablation, numerically exceeding the Frontier Heuristic's $38.6\%$ median
in the separate, 10-episode-per-condition Protocol~A, but remaining below
the Heuristic's $57.0\%$ single-pass result in Protocol~B (exploratory,
$n{=}1$, not statistically matched to this ablation). Because these three
figures come from different checkpoints, training budgets, and evaluation
protocols, none of these comparisons should be read as a direct head-to-head
estimate -- we report them side by side only to note that HighDiscovery's
gain does not obviously close the gap to the heuristic under either
protocol, not to claim a precise margin. We have not evaluated
HighDiscovery beyond a 15-iteration budget or beyond \texttt{cafe}. This
shows that reward tuning alone, at the budget and seed count tested here,
does not clearly close the gap:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structural knowledge advantage:} The Frontier Heuristic
    leverages an explicit occupancy grid, A* graph search, and Voronoi
    bidding to systematically dispatch robots to unexplored clusters. MAPPO,
    relying on local 24-bin LiDAR ranges and an MLP actor, must learn both
    mapping and coordinated pathfinding simultaneously from reward gradients.
    \item \textbf{Training budget limits:} At $3.15\times 10^4$ transitions
    (45 iterations) and $1.05\times 10^4$ transitions (15 iterations), our
    budget reflects the compute constraints of this study's experimental
    setup, not a claim about what budget is typical or sufficient more
    broadly. We cannot rule out that asymptotic training ($>10^6$ transitions) with
    curriculum learning or reward normalization would eventually escape the
    local optimum.
\end{itemize}

A critical distinction highlighted by our protocols is in-distribution
learning versus cross-world transfer. MAPPO was trained exclusively in
\texttt{cafe}. Its performance in \texttt{warehouse}, \texttt{depot},
\texttt{office}, and \texttt{maze} represents zero-shot transfer across
drastically different floor plans (from an open $30\times 50$\,m hall to

\label{sec:limitations}
We summarize the primary methodological and empirical limitations of this study:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Training budget bounds:} The MAPPO policy was trained for 45 iterations ($3.15\times 10^4$ transitions) in Protocol~A and 15 iterations ($1.05\times 10^4$ transitions) in Protocol~C. While sufficient to reveal the penalty-dominated low-displacement mode, we cannot establish whether asymptotic training ($>10^6$ transitions) or dense curriculum shaping would overcome this local optimum.
    \item \textbf{Controller information asymmetry:} The Frontier Heuristic relies on global occupancy grid mapping, frontier clustering, and 8-connected A* search, whereas MAPPO is restricted to local 24-bin LiDAR ranges and random safe waypoints (Table~\ref{tab:controller-assumptions}). Our comparison evaluates the performance gap between an engineered, map-aware controller and a learned local reactive policy, rather than an information-symmetric benchmark.
    \item \textbf{Single-domain training and zero-shot transfer:} The MAPPO policy was trained exclusively in \texttt{cafe}. Performance in \texttt{warehouse}, \texttt{depot}, \texttt{office}, and \texttt{maze} represents zero-shot cross-world generalization ($n{=}1$, exploratory) rather than per-environment specialized learning.
    \item \textbf{Safety filter intervention dynamics:} While all controllers share an identical OSQP unicycle Control Barrier Function (CBF) filter, intervention frequency, duration, and magnitude were not logged. We cannot rule out that the CBF filter selectively overrides MAPPO's requested velocities more frequently than the heuristic's.
    \item \textbf{Statistical sample size:} Resource constraints restricted the paired ablation to $n=3$ independent training seeds. Consequently, neither reward manipulation reaches conventional significance ($\alpha = 0.05$), and the results must be interpreted as diagnostic and exploratory rather than definitive.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
Under a fully specified, compute-bounded training protocol, MAPPO
consistently exhibited low-displacement behavior on multi-robot area
coverage across every evaluation episode we ran; we use ``consistently
observed'' rather than ``reproducible'' deliberately, since we also found
that this evaluation harness is not numerically reproducible at the level
of individual episode outcomes (Section~\ref{sec:nondeterminism}), even
though the qualitative behavior was. Attempting to isolate the failure's
cause via a three-arm reward ablation, we made and then caught a
statistical error: an initial analysis treating each arm's 9 repeated
evaluation episodes as independent samples (pseudoreplication, since only
3 of those 9 per arm come from independent trained checkpoints) suggested
strengthening the discovery bonus produced a ``statistically supported''
coverage gain while weakening the collision penalty did not. The
corrected analysis, using the trained checkpoint (one per seed, $n=3$) as
the unit of independence, shows \emph{neither} manipulation reaches
conventional significance (LowPenalty: mean ACR $28.0\%\to31.0\%$,
paired $t=4.11$, $p=0.054$; HighDiscovery: mean ACR $28.0\%\to40.5\%$,
paired $t=2.93$, $p=0.10$) -- and, strikingly, reverses which arm is
numerically closer to significance, since LowPenalty's smaller effect was
consistent across all three seeds while HighDiscovery's larger effect was
driven by two of three. We regard catching and correcting this error,
rather than allowing the more publication-flattering pseudoreplicated
result to stand, as itself part of this paper's contribution. A
descriptive coverage-efficiency metric (drawn from the same three seeds,
and so not an independent test) does show HighDiscovery moving more
purposefully per metre travelled than the other two arms, which we
report as suggestive rather than confirmatory evidence against a pure
penalty-avoidance account of the original failure mode.
Nonetheless, the classical Frontier Heuristic retained superior performance
across five diverse environments ($1.2\times$ to $2.0\times$ margin on
single-pass, $n{=}1$-per-cell estimates that remain exploratory and were
not re-evaluated in this revision) without requiring policy training.
Under the training budgets, observation structure, and evaluation protocols
tested here, the hand-engineered Frontier Heuristic outperformed the evaluated
MAPPO configuration across all environments tested. We make no direct
sample-efficiency comparison, since we did not construct a training curve for
the heuristic (which requires none) against MAPPO's, nor do we claim that
classical planning universally defeats MARL across all possible architectures.
Rather, for roboticists operating under bounded compute budgets and local
reactive observations, hand-engineered structural coordination remains, on
this evidence, a stronger-performing, training-free alternative. Finally, we
recommend that any follow-up work adopt repeated-episode evaluation per
condition as a baseline requirement, a methodological lesson this paper
learned the hard way.

\section{Infrastructure Faults Encountered During Training}
Two infrastructure faults were encountered and resolved during this work.
First, a segmentation fault inside \texttt{torch.optim.Adam.\_\_init\_\_}
under Ray actor threads was traced to a PyTorch TorchDynamo compilation
hook interacting with Ray's threading model; disabling TorchDynamo
(\texttt{TORCHDYNAMO\_DISABLE=1}) resolved it. Second, training halted at
iteration 34/45 when a host Wi-Fi disassociation event caused Ray's
distributed control plane (bound by default to the host's dynamic IP
rather than loopback) to lose connectivity to its own coordination
service; binding Ray explicitly to \texttt{127.0.0.1} and capping its
object store in-process resolved this and allowed training to resume and
complete to iteration 45. Neither fault altered the training
configuration, reward function, or evaluation protocol reported in the
main text, and we verified the resumed run's hyperparameters and reward
coefficients against the pre-crash configuration; we did not, however,
independently verify that optimizer or RNG internal state was bit-identical
across the interruption, and note this as a residual, unquantified source
of uncertainty in the 45-iteration run specifically (it does not affect
the Protocol~C ablation runs, none of which were interrupted). Both faults
are reported for reproducibility.
