\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Control barrier function (CBF) safety filters are widely used to wrap
learned policies in multi-robot systems, on the premise that the filter
makes the resulting system ``safe.'' We argue that this conflates two
properties: a velocity-level constraint (the filter does not command a
colliding action) and episode-level competence (the policy accomplishes the
task). Using a three-robot ROS~2/Gazebo system with a soft CBF filter and
72 archived evaluation runs, we show empirically that the two come apart.
Competence varied widely across these runs (area coverage from 1.1\% to
98.6\%, although runs differ in world and horizon), yet inter-agent contacts were absent in 69 of 72 runs and were
confined to three swarm-size configurations in the two smallest arenas, so
the collision count carried no information about competence. Within a
27-episode ablation in which coverage ranged from 24\% to 52\%, the
wall-contact count was uncorrelated with coverage (Spearman $\rho=0.04$,
$p=0.85$), and for one checkpoint it varied between 0 and 19 contacts
across repeated evaluations. The filter is also soft: in one 3,500-step run
the minimum inter-robot distance was $0.382\,\si{m}$, below the $0.45\,\si{m}$
configured margin, with no contact. We propose a three-class taxonomy
(competent-and-filtered, incompetent-and-filtered, unfiltered) and a
reporting checklist: a competence metric, the minimum clearance against the
configured margin, the collision definition relative to the filter's own
radius, and repeated evaluation. The evidence comes from one system with
small samples, and we state its limits explicitly.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} control barrier functions, safe reinforcement
learning, multi-robot systems, safety filtering, policy evaluation

\section{Introduction}
A control barrier function (CBF) renders a designated safe set forward
invariant by solving, at each control step, a quadratic program (QP) that
minimally perturbs a nominal control input subject to a barrier
constraint~\cite{ames2019cbf}. In learning-enabled robotics, CBF filters are
often proposed as a way to make an otherwise uncertified learned policy
safe: the policy proposes an action, the filter passes it through or
overrides it, and the composed system is expected to inherit a
collision-avoidance property independent of the policy's
quality~\cite{cheng2019end}. Recent work continues this pattern by enforcing
CBF constraints during RL training rather than only at
deployment~\cite{cbfrl2025}, by combining CBFs with actor-critic
architectures through Koopman representations~\cite{koopmancbf2026}, and by
applying CBF-informed RL to connected and automated
vehicles~\cite{beyondsafety2026} and to mobile robot navigation under
risk~\cite{riskaware2026}.

This literature is framed around a positive claim: the filter makes the
system safe. We do not dispute that a correctly implemented filter bounds
the commanded velocity near detected obstacles. We examine what that does
\emph{not} imply, using a multi-robot system for which the underlying data
are archived and the code is public. Our contribution is narrow. We show
that (i) a collision count is a poor proxy for competence, because it can
be identical for policies whose task performance differs by a large factor;
(ii) a soft CBF filter does not enforce its configured margin as a hard
constraint, so ``zero collisions'' and ``the margin was respected'' are
different statements; and (iii) collision counts in a simulator-in-the-loop
harness can themselves vary between repeated evaluations of the same
checkpoint. We propose a taxonomy and a reporting checklist in response.

\section{Related Work}
Ames et al.~\cite{ames2019cbf} establish CBFs as a general safety
certification tool via forward invariance of a safe set. Cheng et
al.~\cite{cheng2019end} use a barrier-function filter as an online safety
layer around a learned policy for safety-critical continuous control, which
is similar in spirit to the architecture we examine; their emphasis, like
most follow-on work, is on showing that the filter prevents constraint
violation rather than on characterizing what the filtered system can still
do when the policy is weak. CBF-RL~\cite{cbfrl2025} internalizes CBF
constraints into training so that safety need not be enforced online; this
is complementary to our setting, in which an online filter is retained.
Koopman-based CBF filters for actor-critic RL~\cite{koopmancbf2026} and
CBF-informed RL for connected and automated vehicles~\cite{beyondsafety2026}
extend the filtering idea to new system classes. A risk-aware dynamic safety
filter for mobile robot navigation~\cite{riskaware2026} reasons about risk
rather than binary safety. We did not find, in these works, a competence
metric reported alongside the safety metric, but we did not conduct a
systematic review and do not claim that none exists. Henderson et
al.~\cite{henderson2018deep} document how evaluation protocol and seed count
affect conclusions in deep RL; our observation that collision counts vary
between repeated evaluations of one checkpoint is a simulator-level
instance of the same concern. Yu et al.~\cite{yu2022mappo} establish MAPPO
with a centralized critic as a strong baseline for cooperative multi-agent
tasks; we use a policy in this family only as one of the controllers under
test.

\section{System Under Test}
\label{sec:system}
Three TurtleBot3 Waffle robots (differential drive, 24-beam LiDAR) perform
cooperative area coverage in Gazebo Harmonic under ROS~2 Jazzy, in five
worlds (\texttt{cafe}, \texttt{warehouse}, \texttt{depot}, \texttt{office},
\texttt{maze}). Three controllers are evaluated: a Frontier Heuristic
(frontier selection, A* routing, Voronoi partitioning, behavior-tree
recovery), a Random Walk, and a MAPPO policy trained on \texttt{cafe}. All
three issue a nominal command $(v_{\text{nom}},\omega_{\text{nom}})$ to the
same safety stack. Environment, controllers and training are described in a
companion paper~\cite{pandhe2026mappo}; we summarize only what is needed
here.

\textbf{Pre-filter overrides.} Before the CBF solve, the environment applies
two fixed rules to every controller's command. If the minimum LiDAR range
over all beams is below $0.22\,\si{m}$ and the commanded forward speed is
positive, forward speed is replaced by $-0.08\,\si{m/s}$. If another robot
is within $0.45\,\si{m}$ and within $72^\circ$ of the heading, forward speed
is replaced by $-0.05\,\si{m/s}$.

\textbf{Soft CBF filter.} The filter solves, with OSQP,
\begin{equation}
\min_{v,\omega,\delta}\ (v-v_{\text{nom}})^2 + 0.05(\omega-\omega_{\text{nom}})^2
+ P\,\delta^2
\ \text{s.t.}\ A_k v + B_k\omega + \delta \ge -\gamma h_k\ \ \forall k,
\end{equation}
with $|v|\le0.22\,\si{m/s}$, $|\omega|\le1.0\,\si{rad/s}$ and
$0\le\delta\le10$. The barrier terms $h_k$ come from LiDAR returns within
$0.6\,\si{m}$, evaluated at two lookahead points ($\pm l$, $l=0.12\,\si{m}$)
on the heading axis with obstacle radius $d_{\text{safe,obs}}=0.20\,\si{m}$,
and from neighbors within $1.2\,\si{m}$ with agent radius
$d_{\text{safe,agent}}=0.45\,\si{m}$; $\gamma=2.0$ and $P=1000$. After the
solve, forward speed is set to $\min(0,v)$ if any return in the forward cone
(five beams) is below $0.24\,\si{m}$. If the QP is infeasible the filter
returns $(\min(0,v_{\text{nom}}),\omega_{\text{nom}})$. Because of the slack
variable $\delta$, the barrier constraints may be violated at a price, so
\emph{the configured radii are soft}. The agent term uses a relative-distance
barrier that treats the neighbor's velocity as bounded and unmodeled rather
than estimating it. We therefore make no formal safety claim for this
filter; everything below is an empirical observation about its behaviour in
simulation.

\textbf{Collision metrics.} A \emph{wall contact} is counted when the
minimum LiDAR range is below $0.14\,\si{m}$, the approximate sensor-to-bumper
distance of the robot. An \emph{agent contact} is counted when two robots
are closer than $0.20\,\si{m}$ after the first 15 steps of an episode. Note
that the agent-contact threshold ($0.20\,\si{m}$) is much smaller than the
filter's agent margin ($0.45\,\si{m}$): the metric does not test whether the
margin was respected.

\label{sec:empirical}

Each run is a single episode of a single configuration unless stated
otherwise.

Agent contacts occurred in 3 of 20 configurations, all at $N\ge5$ in the two
smallest arenas (\texttt{cafe}, $17\times8\,\si{m}$, and \texttt{office}),
consistent with the fixed margins occupying a larger share of free space as
density rises, though we did not test that explanation. Three longer
single-controller runs (24{,}000-step \texttt{warehouse}, 3{,}500-step
\texttt{depot}, 3{,}500-step \texttt{office}) recorded no agent contact.
Across the 25-run controller matrix and the 27-episode ablation described
below, no agent contact occurred. In total, 69 of the 72 archived sweep,
matrix and ablation runs had no agent contact.

\subsection{The margin is soft}
In the 3{,}500-step \texttt{office} run the minimum inter-robot distance was
$0.382\,\si{m}$, below the $0.45\,\si{m}$ agent margin, with no agent contact
(the contact threshold is $0.20\,\si{m}$). The \texttt{depot} run's minimum
was $0.552\,\si{m}$ and the 24{,}000-step \texttt{warehouse} run's was
$0.814\,\si{m}$. This is the behaviour expected of a soft constraint; it
means that a zero-contact record, taken alone, does not show that the
configured margin was maintained.

\subsection{Dynamic hazards}
Two scenarios were run once each in Gazebo with a scripted moving robot as
the hazard, with the obstacle radius set to $0.22\,\si{m}$ (the dynamic
test script's setting, which differs slightly from the $0.20\,\si{m}$ used
elsewhere). In a head-on approach at a closing speed of $0.38\,\si{m/s}$ the
filter shed forward speed and reversed, with minimum centre distance
$0.349\,\si{m}$, minimum LiDAR range $0.211\,\si{m}$ and no contact. In an
orthogonal crossing at $0.22\,\si{m/s}$ the robot yielded to standstill and
then resumed, with minimum centre distance $0.437\,\si{m}$ and no contact.
Solver-level unit tests with synthetic LiDAR returns check that forward
speed is shed as an approaching return closes (included in the repository's
CI). Each Gazebo scenario is a single run and the raw traces are not
archived, so these are illustrations, not statistical evidence.

\subsection{Collision counts do not track competence}
\label{sec:decouple}
The 25-run controller matrix (5 worlds $\times$ 5 controller/scenario
conditions, one episode each, 150 steps) spans coverage from $1.2\%$ to
$57.0\%$ and contains no agent contact. In \texttt{cafe} the Frontier
Heuristic (57.0\% coverage), Random Walk (22.6\%) and nominal MAPPO
(28.2\%) all recorded zero wall and zero agent contacts. The 27-episode
ablation re-evaluates three MAPPO checkpoints trained under different reward
coefficients, three seeds with three repeated evaluations each (the
repeated evaluations of one checkpoint are not independent samples; see the
companion paper). Coverage ranged from $24.0\%$ to $51.6\%$. Agent contacts
were zero in all 27 episodes, and the wall-contact count was uncorrelated
with coverage (Spearman $\rho=0.04$, $p=0.85$, $n=27$; Pearson $r=-0.04$).
The mean wall-contact count per episode was $4.00$, $0.78$ and $0.00$ for
the arms with mean coverage of $28.0\%$, $31.0\%$ and $40.5\%$, but the
arm difference is driven by a few episodes: for the Baseline checkpoint
the count was 0 in 6 of 9 episodes and 6, 19 and 11 in the other three
(all from one evaluation pass). Repeated evaluation of an identical
checkpoint and seed argument therefore changes collision counts as well as
coverage, which we attribute to real-time simulator timing rather than to
policy sampling, since evaluation uses greedy actions.

The pattern matters for reporting. A collision count that is identically
zero across policies of very different competence cannot rank them, and a
count that varies between 0 and 19 across repeated evaluations of one
checkpoint is not a stable property of the policy.

\section{A Taxonomy and Reporting Checklist}
We classify a filtered learned system along two axes: whether a safety
filter is active, and policy competence under it (a task metric such as
coverage rate or goal-reach rate).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Class A, competent and filtered.} The policy accomplishes the
        task and the filter rarely binds.
  \item \textbf{Class B, incompetent and filtered.} The policy performs
        poorly while the filter prevents constraint violations. A collision
        metric alone cannot distinguish this from Class A.
  \item \textbf{Class C, unfiltered.} Safety and competence are entangled and
        any low collision rate is attributable to the policy.
\end{itemize}
In our data, policies spanning a wide coverage range all fall under the same
zero agent-contact record (Section~\ref{sec:decouple}); on the collision
metric the weaker and stronger ones are indistinguishable. We therefore
recommend that papers reporting collision rates for CBF-filtered learned
policies also report:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a task-competence metric alongside the collision metric;
  \item the minimum clearance achieved, against the configured margin;
  \item the collision definition and its threshold relative to the filter's
        own radius;
  \item the filter's intervention frequency and magnitude per controller
        (we did \emph{not} measure this, which is a limitation of our own
        study); and
  \item repeated evaluation of each checkpoint, with the unit of
        statistical independence stated.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}
The evidence comes from one system, three controllers and a simulator whose
evaluation is not deterministic: three evaluations of an identical
checkpoint and seed produced different results in the companion study, and
the single-episode cells in Table~\ref{tab:sweep} and in the controller
matrix inherit that variance. Sample sizes are small, so we report counts
and one rank correlation rather than significance tests on the sweep or
matrix. The dynamic-hazard results are single runs. The filter is soft and
we offer no formal guarantee. We did not measure how often or by how much
the filter and the pre-filter overrides modify each controller's commands,
so we cannot say how much of any controller's behaviour they mediate. We do
not claim that CBF filters are ineffective: the filter and overrides
operated as configured throughout, and a velocity-level bound is a useful
property in its own right.

An earlier version of this paper reported a frozen, undertrained MAPPO
policy with zero displacement and a higher wall-contact count than two
baselines as its central example. We withdrew it: it came from an earlier
filter implementation (a SciPy SLSQP solver and different collision
thresholds), its raw records are not archived, and it cannot be reproduced
with the current code. The thesis here rests instead on the archived runs
above.

\section{Conclusion}
In a three-robot Gazebo system with a soft CBF filter, agent contacts were
absent in 69 of 72 archived runs while coverage ranged from 1.1\% to
98.6\% (across worlds and horizons), wall-contact counts were uncorrelated with coverage within an
ablation, and the configured agent margin was not strictly maintained. A
collision count therefore cannot stand in for competence, and a zero-contact
record does not show that a soft margin held. We propose reporting a
competence metric, minimum clearance, the collision definition and repeated
evaluations alongside any collision rate, and we note that our own study
lacks the filter-intervention measurement that we recommend.
